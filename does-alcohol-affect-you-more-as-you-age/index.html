Does alcohol affect you more as you age?

If two glasses of wine now leave you feeling the way three or four used to, you're not imagining it. As you get older, the same drinks hit harder — and the reasons are physical, not a matter of willpower.

Plenty of Australians who drank comfortably for decades find that, somewhere past their 50s and 60s, alcohol starts to feel stronger, linger longer and take more out of them the next day. That's a normal part of ageing, and understanding why it happens is the first step to staying safe and still enjoying a drink.

Why the same drink does more with age

Three big changes stack up as you get older, and together they mean a given number of standard drinks pushes your blood alcohol concentration (BAC) higher and keeps it there longer.

1. Less body water to dilute the alcohol

Alcohol spreads through the water in your body, not the fat. As we age we tend to lose muscle and gain fat, and total body water drops. With less water to dilute it, the same amount of alcohol reaches a higher concentration in your blood. According to the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), reduced muscle mass and body water mean some older adults reach a higher BAC than younger people after drinking exactly the same amount.

2. A slower liver

Your liver does around 90% of the work of clearing alcohol, at a roughly fixed pace of about one standard drink an hour. With age, blood flow through the liver decreases and the organ becomes less efficient, so alcohol is processed more slowly and stays in your system longer. The Alcohol and Drug Foundation notes the liver becomes weaker with age, which raises the risk of harm from the same drinking.

3. A more sensitive brain and body

Older people are more sensitive to alcohol's sedative effects and to its impact on balance, coordination, attention and reaction time. The result is a bigger hit to steadiness and judgement — a major reason alcohol is linked to falls, injuries and crashes in older adults, per the NIAAA.

The short version: more concentrated in your blood, cleared more slowly, and felt more strongly by your brain. Same drinks, bigger effect.

Medications: the biggest hidden risk

This is the factor that catches the most people out. Older adults are far more likely to take regular medications, and many of them do not mix well with alcohol. healthdirect warns that alcohol can change how a medicine works, make side effects worse, or become harmful in combination.

The most serious combinations involve medicines that make you drowsy. Mixing alcohol with sedatives, sleeping tablets, benzodiazepines or opioid painkillers can cause excessive sedation — and in some cases can be life-threatening. Alcohol can also interact with some blood pressure medicines, diabetes medicines and antidepressants.

Before you drink on any medication, read the label and the consumer medicine information, and ask your pharmacist or GP. It's a quick conversation, and pharmacists have it every day — there's nothing awkward about asking.

Health conditions alcohol can worsen

Because the risk of chronic conditions rises with age, alcohol has more to act on. Drinking can cause or worsen problems including high blood pressure and heart disease, several cancers, and it's a recognised risk factor for dementia and memory problems. Alcohol can also disrupt sleep and mood — effects that tend to be felt more sharply later in life.

Why this matters for driving

Here's the trap: if you're reaching a higher BAC than you used to on the same drinks, the drinking habit that once kept you under 0.05 might not any more. In Australia the limit is under 0.05 for full-licence holders and zero for learner and provisional drivers. Alcohol also clears more slowly as you age, so the morning-after risk grows too — a big night can leave you over the limit well into the next day.

Estimates are only estimates. No app or rule of thumb can tell you that you're safe to drive, and age adds extra uncertainty to any calculation. If you've been drinking, the safe choice is not to drive.

How much is OK?

The Australian guidelines — no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day — apply to all healthy adults. But because older adults are more sensitive to alcohol and more likely to be on medication, the guidelines specifically suggest speaking with a health professional about an amount that's right for you. For many older Australians, "a bit less than I used to" is a sensible starting point.

Drinking more safely as you get older

  • Count your standard drinks. A generous home pour of wine can be close to two standard drinks — so what feels like "a couple" may be four.
  • Pace yourself. Aim for no more than about one standard drink an hour, and have a glass of water between drinks.
  • Never drink on an empty stomach. Eating before and while you drink slows absorption and softens the peak.
  • Check your medications. Run your list past your pharmacist or GP before drinking.
  • Have several alcohol-free days a week. They give your liver a break and make it easier to stay within the guidelines.
  • Mind your footing. If you feel unsteady after a drink or two, that's your body telling you it's had enough.
None of this means you have to stop. Most older adults can still enjoy a drink — the key is recognising that your body responds differently now, and adjusting the amount and pace to match.

Frequently asked questions

Why does alcohol affect you more as you age?

Your body holds less water and more fat, and your liver works more slowly. Less water means a higher BAC from the same drinks, and a slower liver means alcohol lingers — so the same amount has a bigger effect than it did years ago.

Do older adults reach a higher BAC on the same drinks?

Often, yes. With reduced muscle mass and body water, some older adults reach a higher BAC than younger people after drinking the same amount. The pattern you had at 30 can put you over 0.05 at 65.

Is it safe to drink alcohol while taking medication?

Many common medicines interact with alcohol, and combining it with sedatives, benzodiazepines or opioids can be dangerous. Always check the label and ask your pharmacist or GP.

How much alcohol is safe for older adults?

The guidelines of 10 standard drinks a week and 4 in a day apply to all healthy adults, but older adults are advised to talk with a health professional about a level that suits them.

Does alcohol increase the risk of falls in older people?

Yes. Greater sensitivity to alcohol's effects on balance and coordination raises the risk of falls and injuries — and that risk climbs further alongside sedating medicines.
