Does alcohol raise blood sugar?

It's a trick question. A sugary drink can send your blood sugar up — but alcohol itself tends to push it down, sometimes hours later. Here's what's actually happening, and why it matters most for anyone with diabetes.

The short answer: it can do both

Alcohol's effect on your blood sugar depends on what you drink, how much, and whether you've eaten. Broadly, two things happen at once and pull in opposite directions.

The sugar in your drink raises blood glucose. Sweet wines, cider, regular soft-drink mixers and creamy cocktails carry a real carbohydrate load, so they lift blood sugar much like any sugary drink would.

The alcohol itself lowers blood glucose. Your liver normally trickles stored glucose into your bloodstream between meals to keep levels steady. When there's alcohol on board, the liver drops that job and prioritises breaking the alcohol down instead. With less glucose being released, blood sugar can fall — and keep falling for hours after your last drink.

Put those together and a big, sugary night can look like a spike followed by a crash: up while the sugar hits, then down as the alcohol keeps your liver busy.

Why alcohol can cause a "hypo"

A low blood sugar episode — below about 4 mmol/L — is called hypoglycaemia, or a "hypo". Because your liver stops topping up your glucose while it clears alcohol, drinking makes a hypo more likely, and the effect can be badly delayed: often many hours after you stop, sometimes overnight or into the next day.

This matters far more for some people than others. According to Diabetes Australia, the risk is highest for people taking insulin or the sulfonylurea class of diabetes tablets, where alcohol can trigger a hypo many hours after drinking. Drinking on an empty stomach, or when your blood sugar is already low, adds to the risk.

The dangerous part: a hypo looks like being drunk. Slurred speech, drowsiness, confusion, unsteadiness and irritability are all signs of a hypo — and all things people expect from someone who's been drinking. That overlap means an alcohol-related hypo can be missed by you and by everyone around you. A hypo needs fast-acting carbohydrate, not "sleeping it off".

If you have diabetes: drinking more safely

Most people with diabetes can still enjoy a small amount of alcohol, but it's worth talking it through with your diabetes healthcare team first. Diabetes Australia and the National Diabetes Services Scheme suggest some practical habits:

  • Never drink on an empty stomach. Have alcohol with a meal or carbohydrate-containing food.
  • Choose low-joule or diet mixers — diet cola, diet ginger ale, diet tonic, soda water — rather than sugary ones.
  • Pick lower-alcohol drinks. A light or mid-strength beer is a better choice than a strong one; dry wine over sweet.
  • Drink water between drinks to pace yourself and stay hydrated.
  • Keep a hypo treatment on hand (fast-acting carbs), and make sure someone with you knows how to spot and treat a hypo.
  • Check your blood glucose before, during and after drinking — and again before bed, since a hypo can come overnight.
  • Wear or carry diabetes ID so you get the right help if a hypo is mistaken for being drunk.

For the signs of a hypo and how to treat one, healthdirect's hypoglycaemia guide is a good plain-language reference. This article is general information, not medical advice — your diabetes team's guidance always comes first.

What about people without diabetes?

If you don't have diabetes, your body is generally good at keeping blood sugar within range, and a couple of drinks with a meal won't send it anywhere dramatic. But the same mechanism still applies: heavy drinking, especially on an empty stomach, can drop your blood sugar low enough to leave you shaky, sweaty, foggy and irritable — a state that's easy to mistake for a hangover.

Over the long term there's a bigger-picture link too. Regular heavy drinking is associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes, largely through weight gain, raised blood pressure, and strain on the liver and pancreas. Alcohol is also high in kilojoules, which works against blood-sugar and weight goals at the same time.

The bottom line

"Does alcohol raise blood sugar?" doesn't have a single answer. The sugar in your glass can lift it; the alcohol tends to lower it, sometimes hours later. For most people that balance is harmless in moderation. For anyone using insulin or sulfonylureas, it's a real hazard worth planning around — because the low that alcohol causes can arrive late and wear the disguise of a big night out.

Whatever your situation, the safest frame is the Australian guideline of no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day — fewer is better, and none is fine.

A note on driving: both alcohol and a hypo impair you, and they can strike together. In Australia the legal limit for full-licence drivers is under 0.05, and zero for learner and probationary drivers. Your blood alcohol only falls at about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds it up — so if you've been drinking, don't drive, and treat any estimate as a rough guide, never a green light.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol raise or lower blood sugar?

Both, depending on the drink. Sugary mixers, sweet wines, cider and creamy cocktails push it up; alcohol itself tends to lower it because your liver stops releasing stored glucose while it clears the alcohol. A sugary drink can spike your blood sugar and then crash it hours later.

Can drinking alcohol cause a hypo?

Yes — and the effect can be delayed for many hours, sometimes overnight. The risk is highest for people on insulin or sulfonylurea tablets, and when drinking on an empty stomach.

Why is an alcohol-related hypo dangerous?

Its symptoms look almost identical to being drunk, so it's easily missed. A hypo needs fast-acting carbohydrate, not sleeping it off.

What are the best drinks for steady blood sugar?

Lower-alcohol options with little added sugar: light or mid-strength beer, dry wine, or spirits with a diet mixer, soda water or water. Avoid sugary mixers, sweet wines, cider and creamy cocktails, and don't drink on an empty stomach.

Can drinking alcohol cause diabetes?

Not type 1. Heavy long-term drinking can raise type 2 risk indirectly, through weight gain, raised blood pressure and liver and pancreas damage. Staying within the guidelines lowers that risk.
