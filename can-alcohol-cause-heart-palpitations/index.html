Can alcohol cause heart palpitations?

Yes — a racing, fluttering or pounding heartbeat during or after drinking is common, and a heavy session can trigger a genuine burst of irregular rhythm known as "holiday heart". Here's what's happening and when it's serious.

You've had a big night, you're lying in bed, and your heart won't settle — it's thumping, skipping, or fluttering like it's trying to catch up with itself. It's an unsettling feeling, and a very common one. Alcohol is one of the best-known triggers for heart palpitations, and for some people a single heavy session is enough to tip the heart into a temporary irregular rhythm.

What are heart palpitations?

Palpitations are simply the sensation of being aware of your own heartbeat. It might feel like your heart is racing, pounding, fluttering, skipping a beat, or beating irregularly. Most palpitations are harmless and pass on their own, but they can also be a sign of an abnormal heart rhythm (an arrhythmia). Common triggers include caffeine, stress, lack of sleep, nicotine — and alcohol. Australian health service healthdirect lists limiting alcohol and caffeine as a first step in managing palpitations.

How alcohol sets your heart racing

Alcohol affects the heart's rhythm in several overlapping ways, especially at higher doses:

  • It fires up your "fight or flight" system. Drinking — and the hours of processing that follow — raises adrenaline and stress hormones, which speed up the heart and make it more excitable.
  • It disturbs the heart's electrical signalling. Alcohol and its by-products can interfere with the electrical impulses that keep the upper and lower chambers of the heart beating in time.
  • It dehydrates you and shifts your electrolytes. Alcohol is a diuretic, and low levels of minerals like potassium and magnesium after a heavy night make the heart more prone to skipped or extra beats.
  • It wrecks your sleep. Broken, shallow sleep keeps stress hormones elevated — which is partly why palpitations so often hit at 3am or first thing in the morning.

Add caffeine, a late night, and the stress of a big event on top, and you have a recipe for a fluttering heart.

"Holiday heart syndrome": when it's more than a flutter

Sometimes a heavy session pushes the heart past a harmless flutter into a genuine rhythm disturbance called atrial fibrillation (AF or AFib). Doctors nicknamed this "holiday heart syndrome" back in 1978, after noticing a pattern of otherwise healthy people turning up with palpitations after weekends, celebrations and holidays spent drinking heavily.

In atrial fibrillation, the heart's upper chambers (the atria) quiver instead of squeezing properly, and fall out of sync with the lower chambers. The result is a fast, irregular heartbeat. As Australian experts explain in this overview of holiday heart, the striking thing is that it can happen to people with no diagnosed heart disease at all.

Symptoms of an AF episode can include:

  • A racing, irregular or pounding heartbeat (palpitations)
  • Chest discomfort or tightness
  • Shortness of breath
  • Dizziness or light-headedness
  • Tiredness or weakness, or feeling faint

The good news is that a holiday-heart episode usually settles within about 24 hours once you stop drinking and the alcohol clears your system. The catch: it can return with the next heavy session, and repeated episodes can be an early warning of a longer-term rhythm problem.

How much does it take?

There's no proven "safe" amount when it comes to alcohol and heart rhythm — and the effect isn't limited to heavy drinkers. A 2021 study led by researchers at the University of California, San Francisco tracked people's drinking in real time using heart monitors and skin alcohol sensors. It found that:

Alcohol in prior ~4 hoursOdds of an AF episode
1 drinkRoughly 2× higher
2 or more drinksMore than 3× higher

In other words, even one drink measurably raised the short-term risk of an episode in people prone to AF, and the risk climbed with each additional drink. Binge drinking — four or more standard drinks in one sitting — carries the highest risk of tipping the heart into an irregular rhythm.

The flip side is encouraging. For people who already get atrial fibrillation, cutting alcohol out makes a real difference. A 2020 clinical trial found that regular drinkers with AF who gave up alcohol had noticeably fewer episodes over the following six months than those who kept drinking. If your heart flutters after a big night, less alcohol genuinely means fewer flutters.

Who's most at risk

Anyone can get holiday heart, but the odds are higher if you:

  • Drink heavily in a short time (binge drinking), even occasionally
  • Already have high blood pressure or a known heart condition
  • Have had palpitations or atrial fibrillation before
  • Are older — AF becomes more common with age
  • Are sleep-deprived, stressed, dehydrated, or piling caffeine on top of alcohol

The Heart Foundation notes that heavy and binge drinking raises the risk of atrial fibrillation, and that people who already have AF may need to drink less — or not at all.

What to do if your heart is racing after drinking

For a short-lived flutter with no other symptoms, the sensible steps are to stop drinking for the night, sip water, avoid caffeine, and rest. Slow, steady breathing can help calm things down. Most harmless palpitations pass on their own as the alcohol clears.

When to get help. See a doctor if palpitations keep coming back, last a long time, or you feel unwell with them. Call 000 immediately if a racing or irregular heartbeat comes with chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or you feel like you might collapse — that is a medical emergency, not something to sleep off.

Drinking with a healthier heart rhythm in mind

You don't have to give up entirely to be kinder to your heart — pacing and staying under the guidelines does most of the work. Australia's NHMRC guidelines recommend no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day to keep health risks low. Practical steps that also protect your rhythm:

  • Avoid binge sessions — spread drinks out and cap the night at a number you decide in advance.
  • Alternate every drink with water to stay hydrated and slow the pace.
  • Eat before and while you drink.
  • Go easy on the coffee and energy drinks on a big night — caffeine plus alcohol is a double hit for palpitations.
  • Keep some alcohol-free days each week.

Tracking how much you actually drink is a simple way to stay under the limits and spot the sessions that leave your heart complaining the next day.

Frequently asked questions

Can drinking alcohol cause heart palpitations?

Yes. Alcohol can disrupt the heart's normal rhythm, and a fluttering, racing or pounding heartbeat during or after drinking is common. A heavy session can trigger a burst of atrial fibrillation — "holiday heart" — even in people with no known heart problem.

What is holiday heart syndrome?

It's an episode of atrial fibrillation brought on by heavy or binge drinking in an otherwise healthy person, first described by doctors in 1978.

How much alcohol triggers atrial fibrillation?

There's no proven safe amount. Research found one drink roughly doubled the short-term odds of an AF episode and two or more drinks more than tripled them, with risk rising the more you drink.

How long do alcohol-related palpitations last?

A holiday-heart episode usually settles within about 24 hours once you stop drinking. It can return with future heavy sessions, and repeated episodes are worth checking with a doctor.

When should I see a doctor?

If palpitations keep happening or last a long time, see your GP. Call 000 for palpitations with chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting.

A note on driving: in Australia the legal limit for full-licence drivers is under 0.05, and zero for learner and probationary drivers. No app or estimate can tell you that you're safe to drive — and if you've been drinking, don't. This article is general information, not medical advice; see your doctor about your own heart health.
