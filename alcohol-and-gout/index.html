Alcohol and gout: why beer triggers flare-ups

Alcohol is one of the biggest dietary risk factors for gout — it pushes up the uric acid in your blood and can set off an attack within a day. And when it comes to gout, beer is the worst drink of the lot. Here's why, and what you can do about it.

What gout actually is

Gout is the most common form of inflammatory arthritis. It happens when there's too much urate (uric acid) in your blood — either because your kidneys aren't clearing enough of it, or your body is making too much. Over months and years, that excess urate can form tiny, needle-like crystals in and around a joint.

A gout attack is your immune system reacting to those crystals: sudden, severe pain, swelling, redness and heat, most often in the big toe, though it can hit the ankle, knee, foot or hand. According to healthdirect Australia, an attack comes on fast and can last one to two weeks. Gout is much more common in men and in older adults, and rare in women before menopause.

How alcohol feeds a gout attack

Alcohol raises your uric acid through several mechanisms at once, which is why it hits gout from more than one angle:

  • It blocks uric acid from leaving. When your liver breaks down alcohol it produces lactate (lactic acid). Lactate competes with urate for the same exit route through the kidneys, so less uric acid gets flushed out in your urine and more stays in your blood.
  • It speeds up uric acid production. Alcohol accelerates the breakdown of purines (the building blocks of DNA) into uric acid, adding to the pile from the other direction.
  • Beer brings its own purines. Beer is made with brewer's yeast, which is especially high in purines. So beer delivers a double hit — the alcohol plus a direct load of the raw material your body turns into urate.
  • It dehydrates you. Alcohol is a diuretic, so it makes you pass more fluid. Dehydration concentrates the uric acid already in your blood, which can help tip crystals into forming.

Which drinks are worst for gout?

Not all drinks carry the same risk. A landmark 12-year study of 47,150 men (Choi and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2004) tracked who went on to develop gout and found a clear ranking by drink type. Each extra daily serving changed the risk like this:

DrinkRisk per daily servingWhy
Beer~49% higherAlcohol plus purines from brewer's yeast
Spirits~15% higherAlcohol effect, no significant purine load
Wine (moderate)No significant increaseLower purines; protective plant compounds may offset the alcohol

The researchers summed it up plainly: beer confers a larger risk than spirits, while moderate wine drinking did not raise the risk. That doesn't make wine a health tonic — heavier wine drinking still adds alcohol, and alcohol in any form raises your overall risk. But litre for litre, beer is the standout problem.

Beer isn't the "safe" low-strength option here. People often reach for beer thinking it's gentler than spirits because it's weaker. For gout it's the opposite: the purines in beer mean it does more damage per drink, not less. If you're managing gout and want a beer, it counts for more than its alcohol alone suggests.

How much alcohol raises the risk

The same 12-year study showed the risk climbs steadily with the total amount of alcohol, regardless of type. Compared with men who didn't drink at all:

Daily alcoholRough standard drinks*Gout risk vs non-drinkers
10–14.9 g~1–1.5~1.3× (not statistically significant)
15–29.9 g~1.5–3~1.5×
30–49.9 g~3–5~2.0×
50 g or more~5+~2.5×

*An Australian standard drink contains 10 g of pure alcohol.

So someone drinking around five standard drinks a day had roughly two and a half times the risk of developing gout compared with a non-drinker — a dose-response pattern that's hard to explain away.

Can one big night set off an attack?

If you already have gout, yes — and quickly. A separate study published in The American Journal of Medicine followed 197 people with gout and looked at what they'd drunk before each attack. The more alcohol in the previous 24 hours, the higher the odds of a flare:

  • 1–2 drinks in the past day: about 1.4× the odds of an attack
  • 5–6 drinks: about 2.7×
  • 7 or more drinks: about 3.1×

Two things stand out. First, the effect is fast and short-lived — it mostly shows up within a day of drinking, so a heavy session can bring on an attack almost immediately. Second, for triggering an attack in someone who already has gout, it was the total amount of alcohol that mattered, not the specific drink. (Beer's extra purine load matters more for the long-term build-up that causes gout in the first place.)

Drinking (or not) when you have gout

Whether to drink at all with gout is a decision for you and your doctor, but the general advice from bodies like the Mayo Clinic is consistent:

  • Avoid alcohol completely during an attack. Adding alcohol while a joint is already inflamed pours fuel on the fire.
  • Limit it between attacks, and cut beer first.
  • Stay well hydrated. Drinking plenty of water helps your kidneys clear uric acid and counters alcohol's drying effect.
  • Watch the rest of your diet too. Organ meats, some seafood (anchovies, sardines, shellfish) and sugary drinks high in fructose also push up uric acid — alcohol is one piece of a bigger picture.
  • Keep taking prescribed medication. Urate-lowering drugs such as allopurinol work long-term; alcohol can work against them if you overdo it, particularly in the first months of treatment.
This isn't medical advice. Gout is a medical condition, and alcohol can interact with gout medications and with the other health problems that often come alongside it (high blood pressure, kidney disease, type 2 diabetes). If you have gout or frequent attacks, talk to your GP about what's safe for you.

Cutting back without giving up everything

You don't have to go completely dry to lower your risk — though for some people that's the right call. A few practical moves make a real difference:

  • Swap beer for a glass of wine if you're going to drink — it's the single biggest drink-type change you can make for gout.
  • Count your standard drinks rather than glasses, and keep within the Australian guideline of no more than 10 a week and 4 on any day.
  • Match every drink with water to stay hydrated and slow your pace.
  • Build in alcohol-free days. Our guide on how to cut down on drinking has more ways to ease off without feeling like you're missing out.

Tracking what you actually drink is the first step — most people underestimate it, and gout risk rises with every extra daily serving.

A note on driving: alcohol's effect on gout is a separate issue from its effect behind the wheel. In Australia the legal limit for full-licence drivers is under 0.05, and zero for learner and provisional drivers. Your body clears only about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds that up — so if you've been drinking, don't drive.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol cause gout?

It's one of the strongest dietary risk factors, though rarely the only cause. Alcohol raises blood uric acid and can trigger attacks. In the 12-year study of 47,150 men, drinking around five standard drinks a day roughly doubled-to-tripled the risk of developing gout versus not drinking.

Which alcohol is worst for gout?

Beer, because it carries both alcohol and purines. Each daily beer raised gout risk by about 49% in the research, spirits by about 15%, and moderate wine showed no significant increase.

Can one night of drinking trigger a gout attack?

Yes. In people who already have gout, 1–2 drinks in the past 24 hours raised the odds of an attack about 1.4×, and 7 or more drinks about 3×. The effect usually appears within a day.

Can I drink alcohol if I have gout?

Ask your doctor. The usual advice is no alcohol during an attack, limited alcohol (especially beer) between attacks, plenty of water, and staying within the national guidelines.

How does alcohol raise uric acid?

Breaking down alcohol produces lactate, which blocks uric acid from leaving via the kidneys; alcohol also speeds purine breakdown into uric acid; and beer adds extra purines. Being a diuretic, alcohol also dehydrates you, concentrating the urate that's left.
