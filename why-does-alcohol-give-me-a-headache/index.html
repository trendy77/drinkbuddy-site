Why does alcohol give me a headache?

A throbbing head is one of the most common reasons people regret last night's drinks. But an alcohol headache isn't one thing — it comes in two distinct forms, and understanding what's driving yours is the first step to heading it off.

For some people, a single glass of red wine is enough to bring on a pounding head within the hour. For others, the pain waits until the next morning, arriving with the rest of the hangover. Both are real, both have names, and both come down to what alcohol does inside your body once you swallow it. Here's what's actually going on — and why the drink you choose matters as much as how much you have.

Two kinds of alcohol headache

Headache specialists recognise two separate types of headache brought on by drinking:

  • The immediate headache — sometimes called a "cocktail headache". It comes on within about 30 minutes to three hours of drinking, while alcohol is still in your system. Some people get it after just one or two drinks.
  • The delayed headache — the classic hangover headache. It shows up 5 to 12 hours later, usually the next morning, as your blood alcohol level falls back towards zero.

The delayed version is technically called a delayed alcohol-induced headache (DAIH). Research describes it as a moderate, pressing headache, typically felt across the front of the head — the familiar morning-after throb. The two types can also stack: a heavy night can hand you both.

What's actually causing the pain

There's no single culprit. Several mechanisms overlap, and different ones dominate depending on the drink and the person.

1. Blood vessels widening

Alcohol is a vasodilator — it makes blood vessels, including those around the head, expand. That shift in blood flow and pressure can stimulate the brain's pain pathways (the trigeminal system), which is thought to be a big part of the immediate, drink-in-hand headache.

2. Dehydration and lost electrolytes

Alcohol suppresses a hormone called vasopressin, the signal that tells your kidneys to hold on to water. With it switched down, you produce more urine and lose fluid faster than you're replacing it. That excess urination also strips out electrolytes like sodium, potassium and magnesium, which your nerves and muscles need to work properly. Dehydration and that electrolyte imbalance are major drivers of the next-day headache. (Our guide on whether alcohol dehydrates you digs into the detail.)

3. Congeners in darker drinks

Congeners are minor compounds produced during fermentation and distilling — they add colour, flavour and aroma. They're far more concentrated in dark drinks like whisky, brandy, dark rum and red wine than in clear ones like vodka or gin. Congeners are known to worsen the severity of hangovers, headache included, and are thought to irritate blood vessels and brain tissue. It's a big reason a night on the dark spirits often hurts more than the same amount of a clear one.

4. Histamines and other wine compounds

Fermented drinks — especially red wine and some beers — contain histamine, the same inflammatory chemical involved in allergic reactions. Alcohol also prompts your body to release more of its own histamine and blunts the enzyme that would normally break it down. Red wine adds another suspect: plant compounds called flavonols, particularly quercetin, which may slow your body's ability to clear acetaldehyde (more on that next), letting this headache-linked toxin build up.

5. Acetaldehyde and acetate — alcohol's by-products

Your body breaks alcohol down in stages. First it becomes acetaldehyde, a toxic compound that's far nastier than the alcohol itself, then it's converted to acetate and finally to water and carbon dioxide. Both middle products are linked to feeling awful. In fact, animal research points to acetate — the later by-product, which peaks as your blood alcohol returns to zero — as a key trigger of the delayed hangover headache, which helps explain why the worst of the pain often lands the morning after, not while you're drinking.

Why red wine is the usual suspect

If one drink gets blamed for headaches more than any other, it's red wine. Around a third of people who get migraines name alcohol as a trigger, and among them red wine is cited most often. It's a perfect storm: higher histamine than white or sparkling wine, a rich load of flavonols like quercetin, tannins, plus the general vasodilating and dehydrating effects of the alcohol itself.

What's striking is that studies suggest red wine can set off a headache independent of how much you drink in susceptible people — a small glass can be enough. That said, the science is still untangling exactly which compound does what, and sensitivity varies hugely from person to person. If you've noticed a pattern with a particular drink, that observation is worth trusting.

Migraine and alcohol. If you live with migraines, you may react to far less alcohol than someone who doesn't. Research shows people prone to migraine can get a delayed alcohol headache from a modest amount, while others need a lot more to feel it. A prior history of headaches is one of the strongest predictors of getting one after drinking. If alcohol reliably triggers your migraines, avoiding it — or at least your known trigger drinks — is a legitimate strategy.

How to avoid an alcohol headache

You can't change your biology, but you can lower the odds. Most of these come back to giving your body less to deal with and keeping fluids up:

  • Drink less, or skip it. Fewer drinks means less dehydration, fewer congeners and less acetaldehyde — the simplest and most reliable fix.
  • Pace yourself. Around one standard drink an hour gives your body time to keep up. Don't drink on an empty stomach.
  • Match every drink with water. Alternating alcohol with a glass of water directly counters the dehydration driving the morning-after headache.
  • Choose your drink. If you're prone, clear, lower-congener drinks (vodka, gin) tend to hurt less than dark spirits and red wine. Watch red wine in particular.
  • Know your numbers. The Australian guidelines recommend no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day for healthy adults. Treat that as a ceiling, not a target.

If a headache has already landed, the honest answer is that time and rehydration do most of the work. Painkillers can ease a hangover headache, but be cautious — check the label, and be aware that some can be hard on the stomach or liver when you've been drinking. For everyday tactics, the government's healthdirect tips for safe drinking and the NHMRC alcohol guidelines are good starting points, and Harvard Health has a clear rundown of the link between alcohol and headaches.

When a headache is more than a headache. An occasional alcohol headache is common and not usually cause for concern. But see a doctor if drinking reliably brings on severe or one-sided headaches, if headaches come with other symptoms like a rash, swelling or breathing trouble (which can point to alcohol intolerance rather than a simple headache), or if you're using alcohol to cope and finding it hard to cut back. Persistent or worsening headaches always deserve a medical opinion.
A note on driving: a headache clearing up doesn't mean the alcohol has gone. Whether or not your head hurts, the legal limit for full-licence drivers in Australia is under 0.05%, and zero for learner and probationary drivers. Only time lowers your blood alcohol — roughly one standard drink an hour — so the morning after a big night you can still be over the limit. Any estimate is an estimate, never a green light.

Frequently asked questions

Why does alcohol give me a headache?

Alcohol widens blood vessels, dehydrates you and flushes out electrolytes, and breaks down into a toxic by-product called acetaldehyde. Darker drinks add congeners and fermented drinks add histamines, both of which aggravate blood vessels. Together these produce either an immediate headache within hours or a delayed hangover headache the next day.

Why do I get a headache after only one or two drinks?

You may be prone to the immediate "cocktail headache", which can start within 30 minutes to three hours. People who get migraines are especially sensitive — about a third name alcohol as a trigger — and red wine is the drink most often blamed, thanks to compounds like histamine and quercetin.

Why does red wine give me a headache?

Red wine is higher in histamine than white or sparkling wine and rich in flavonols like quercetin, which may slow how your body clears acetaldehyde. Tannins and the general effects of alcohol add to it. In susceptible people, red wine can trigger a headache regardless of how much they drink.

Why do I wake up with a headache the morning after drinking?

That's the delayed alcohol-induced headache — the hangover headache — which typically appears 5 to 12 hours after drinking as your blood alcohol returns to zero. Dehydration, poor sleep, low blood sugar and the build-up of alcohol's by-products (especially acetate) all contribute.

How do I prevent an alcohol headache?

Drink less or not at all, pace yourself at about one standard drink an hour, alternate every drink with water, don't drink on an empty stomach, and choose clear, lower-congener drinks if you're prone. Staying within the Australian guidelines keeps the risk lower.
