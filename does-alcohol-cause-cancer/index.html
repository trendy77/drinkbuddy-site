Does alcohol cause cancer?

Yes — and the evidence is not new or shaky. Alcohol is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen, the same top tier as tobacco and asbestos, and it's linked to at least seven different cancers. Here's how it actually causes cancer, which cancers it raises the risk of, and why health authorities now say there's no safe level.

The short answer: yes, alcohol causes cancer

The link between alcohol and cancer has been established for decades, but many people still don't know it. The Cancer Council Australia and the Alcohol and Drug Foundation are clear: alcoholic drinks are a Group 1 carcinogen, as classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). That's the highest category of certainty — reserved for things proven to cause cancer in humans, alongside tobacco and asbestos. It doesn't mean alcohol is as dangerous as smoking drink-for-drink; it means the evidence that it causes cancer is just as solid.

The scale is significant. In 2020, around 5,800 new cancer cases in Australia were attributable to alcohol, and in 2021 alcohol-related cancers claimed the lives of about 1,680 Australians. Globally, a 2021 study in The Lancet Oncology linked alcohol to more than 740,000 new cancer cases in a single year — about 4% of all cancers diagnosed worldwide.

Which cancers are linked to alcohol?

Drinking is directly linked to at least seven types of cancer. These are the cancers where the evidence is strongest:

Breast cancer deserves a special mention. It's one of the cancers where risk starts to climb from very low levels of drinking — there's no threshold you have to cross first. Because breast cancer is common, even a small increase in individual risk adds up to a large number of cases across the population.

Alcohol and smoking together are worse than either alone. For cancers of the mouth, throat and oesophagus, drinking and smoking don't just add up — they multiply each other's effect. Alcohol helps the carcinogens in tobacco smoke soak into the lining of your mouth and throat.

How does alcohol cause cancer?

Alcohol doesn't damage the body in just one way. Researchers have identified several mechanisms that work together:

Acetaldehyde and DNA damage

This is the big one. When your body breaks down alcohol, it first converts it into a toxic chemical called acetaldehyde. Acetaldehyde damages your cells and the DNA inside them, causing the kind of mutations that can eventually turn a healthy cell into a cancerous one. It's produced in the mouth, stomach and liver as alcohol is processed.

Oxidative stress and inflammation

Alcohol increases inflammation in the body and generates unstable molecules that damage cells, proteins and DNA — a process known as oxidative stress. Over years, that ongoing damage raises the odds that something goes wrong in the way cells grow and divide.

Hormonal changes

Drinking can raise levels of hormones such as oestrogen. Higher oestrogen is linked to a higher risk of hormone-driven cancers, which is one of the reasons the connection between alcohol and breast cancer is so consistent.

Helping other carcinogens in

Alcohol acts as a kind of solvent in the mouth and throat, making it easier for carcinogens — especially those from tobacco smoke — to be absorbed into the tissues there. This is a major reason drinking and smoking together are so much more harmful than either on its own.

Is there a safe amount?

When it comes to cancer, the honest answer from health authorities is no. In 2023 the World Health Organization stated plainly that no level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health, and that risk to the drinker begins from the very first drop. It doesn't matter whether you drink beer, wine or spirits — what matters is how much and how often.

The risk is dose-related: the more you drink, the higher your risk. An Australian study found that drinking two standard drinks a day increases a woman's cancer risk by about 5% and a man's by about 3%, and that an extra seven drinks a week raises the risk of dying from an alcohol-related cancer by around 12%. Small amounts carry small risks — but not zero risk.

What about "red wine is good for you"? You've probably heard that a glass of red wine has health benefits. More recent research has largely overturned that idea — many of the older studies had flaws, and reviews now conclude that the cancer and other health risks of alcohol outweigh any supposed benefit. There's no cancer upside to drinking.

The good news: cutting down lowers your risk

Because the risk rises with the amount you drink, the reverse is also true — drinking less lowers your risk, and the benefit builds over time. You don't have to quit entirely to help yourself; every drink you don't have is a small reduction in risk. If you do choose to drink, the NHMRC Australian guidelines recommend no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day to keep the risk of alcohol-related disease low.

Practical ways to cut back:

  • Have several alcohol-free days each week. This alone pulls your weekly total down noticeably.
  • Know what a standard drink really is. A large glass of wine or a schooner of full-strength beer is often more than one standard drink, so it's easy to drink more than you realise.
  • Choose low- or no-alcohol options. The zero-alcohol range has grown enormously and lets you keep the ritual without the risk.
  • Keep count. People routinely underestimate how much they drink over a week — tracking it turns a vague guess into a number you can actually act on.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol really cause cancer?

Yes. Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen — the same top evidence category as tobacco and asbestos — meaning there's strong, proven evidence it causes cancer in humans. It's directly linked to at least seven cancers.

Which cancers are linked to alcohol?

At least seven: mouth, throat (pharynx and larynx), oesophagus, stomach, bowel, liver and breast. Risk rises the more you drink, but for breast, mouth and throat cancer even small amounts raise the risk.

Is there a safe amount of alcohol for cancer risk?

No. The World Health Organization says there is no safe level of drinking for our health. Every drink adds some risk, and the type of drink doesn't matter — only how much and how often. Staying within the Australian guidelines lowers, but doesn't remove, the risk.

How does alcohol cause cancer?

Mainly through acetaldehyde, a toxic chemical made when your body breaks down alcohol, which damages cell DNA. It also causes inflammation and oxidative stress, raises oestrogen (breast cancer), and helps other carcinogens absorb in the mouth and throat.

Does cutting down on drinking lower my cancer risk?

Yes. The risk is dose-related, so drinking less means lower risk, and the benefit grows over time. Fewer drinks a week, alcohol-free days and low- or no-alcohol options all help — and tracking your intake makes cutting back much easier.
