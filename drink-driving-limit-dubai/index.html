What's the drink-driving limit in Dubai and the UAE?

By Trendy Publishing editorial team · Published 17 October 2026 · Sources

There isn't one. Unlike Australia's 0.05%, the United Arab Emirates — including Dubai and Abu Dhabi — has a zero-tolerance policy: there is no legal blood alcohol level you're allowed to drive with. Any trace of alcohol at all while you're behind the wheel is a criminal offence. For Australian travellers used to a "one drink is probably fine" limit, that's a genuinely dangerous assumption to carry off the plane.

The limit is zero — there is no legal "one drink"

Australia, the UK and most of Europe set a number — 0.05% here, 0.08% in England — below which driving is legal. The UAE doesn't work that way. The rule is simply that you must not drive with any alcohol in your system. The Australian government's Smartraveller advice for the UAE puts it bluntly: you may be jailed or fined if there is any alcohol in your blood while driving, and these laws are strictly enforced.

That means the familiar mental maths — a couple of light beers over a long dinner, then a careful drive home — has no place here. There's no headroom to calculate against, because the threshold is zero. The only reading that keeps you on the right side of the law is nothing at all.

The penalties are severe

Drink-driving in the UAE is treated as a serious crime, not a traffic ticket. The exact sentence is set by the court, but the framework is harsh and consistent across the emirates.

ConsequenceWhat to expect
Licence suspensionOne year, running from the date of the verdict
FineSet by the court — frequently tens of thousands of dirhams
PrisonPossible; the court decides the jail term
Black pointsCommonly 23 points added to the driving record
VehicleImpounded, commonly for around 60 days
Tourists & expatsA conviction can lead to deportation and a re-entry ban

The one-year suspension and "court decides the fine and/or jail term" come from the UAE Government's own road-safety and traffic-fines portal. On top of the court penalty, a drink-driving conviction will void your travel insurance and any hire-car cover, leaving you personally liable for damage — and for a visitor, the deportation risk makes a single bad decision genuinely trip-ending.

The stopover trap. Dubai and Abu Dhabi are among the most common layovers for Australians flying to Europe. You won't be driving on a stopover — but being intoxicated in public is also an offence, and travellers have been arrested at UAE airports after drinking heavily on the flight or during transit. A free-flowing long-haul does not reset when you land. Pace yourself even on a layover.

You can drink in the UAE — but the rules changed, and driving didn't

It's easy to get the two things tangled. In November 2020 the UAE overhauled its alcohol laws: drinking and possessing alcohol without a personal licence was decriminalised for adults, and tourists can now buy alcohol using their passport. Gulf News has a clear rundown of what the 2020 reform changed. So drinking at your hotel bar or a licensed restaurant is entirely legal and normal.

What the reform did not touch is driving. The zero-tolerance road rule is untouched by the loosening of consumption laws. Being legally allowed to have a few drinks at dinner and being legally allowed to then drive are two completely separate things in the UAE — and the second one simply isn't permitted at any level.

Drinking age and the emirate differences

The UAE is a federation of seven emirates, and alcohol rules vary between them. The drinking age and where you can even buy a drink both depend on which emirate you're in.

EmirateLegal drinking ageNotes
Dubai21Alcohol served in licensed hotels, bars and restaurants
Abu Dhabi18Hotels generally serve only over-21s
Sharjah—Completely dry; alcohol banned, even in hotels
Other Northern Emirates21Licensed venues; rules vary locally

One practical catch: carrying alcohol from one emirate into another can cause trouble, especially crossing into Sharjah, where it's banned outright. And a liquor licence issued in one emirate isn't valid in another. None of this changes the driving rule — zero tolerance applies everywhere in the UAE regardless of which emirate's bar poured the drink.

Public intoxication counts too. Drinking in public places like beaches, parks and streets is illegal, and being drunk in public outside a licensed venue can lead to arrest. If you drink, keep it to licensed venues or private homes, and arrange a taxi or rideshare home rather than walking it off in the street.

Driving in the UAE on an Australian licence

Tourists can generally drive a hire car in the UAE on a valid Australian licence together with an International Driving Permit (apply for the IDP in Australia before you leave; a car-hire company will also tell you what it needs). A foreign licence gives you no leeway whatsoever on alcohol, though — the zero-tolerance rule applies to visitors exactly as it does to residents. Check the current requirements on Smartraveller before you go, and remember that driving with any alcohol in your system voids your insurance and hire-car cover on top of the criminal penalty.

Watch what's in the glass

One more travel-safety point that applies across the region: stick to branded, sealed drinks from licensed venues. Cheap or home-made spirits anywhere overseas can contain methanol, a toxic substance that can't be seen, smelt or tasted and that the World Health Organization warns can cause blindness, organ damage or death. Smartraveller notes that as little as one shot of methanol-contaminated alcohol can be fatal. In the UAE's licensed hotels and bars this is a low risk, but the same caution applies anywhere — if a drink tastes off, stop.

The morning after still counts

Your body clears alcohol slowly — on average only about one standard drink an hour — and nothing speeds it up. Coffee, a big breakfast, a shower and a good sleep can make you feel sharper, but they don't lower your blood alcohol. In the UAE, where the limit is zero, there is no margin for error at all: if you drank late, you could easily still register alcohol the next morning, and that's enough for the offence. After a night out, don't drive the next day until you're certain you're completely clear — or just don't hire the car for those days.

Plenty of countries are strict, but the UAE sits at the hardest end of the scale alongside a short list of zero-tolerance nations. If you're used to Australia's 0.05, the simplest way to adjust is to stop thinking in terms of a limit at all: in the UAE, drinking and driving are two activities that never overlap on the same day.

A note on driving: no app, calculator or rule of thumb can tell you that you're under a limit — let alone safe — to drive. Blood and breath alcohol estimates carry real uncertainty, and in a zero-tolerance country any detectable alcohol is an offence. If you've had a drink in the UAE, don't drive. Use a taxi, a rideshare or the Dubai Metro instead.

Frequently asked questions

What is the drink-driving limit in Dubai and the UAE?

There isn't one — the UAE has a zero-tolerance policy. There's no legal blood alcohol level you can drive with, so any trace of alcohol while driving is a criminal offence. The only safe number is zero.

What are the penalties for drink-driving in the UAE?

It's a criminal offence. A driver under the influence has their licence suspended for a year, and the court decides the fine and/or jail term. Black points and a vehicle impoundment are usually added, and for a tourist or expat it can mean deportation and a re-entry ban.

Can tourists drink alcohol in Dubai?

Yes. Since a 2020 law change, over-21s can drink in licensed hotels, bars and restaurants, and tourists can buy alcohol with their passport. The catch is that you still cannot drive afterwards with any alcohol in your system.

What is the legal drinking age in Dubai?

It's 21 in Dubai and most of the UAE. Abu Dhabi's legal age is 18, though hotels there usually serve only over-21s. Sharjah is completely dry, with alcohol banned even in hotels.

Does the zero-tolerance rule apply on a Dubai stopover?

The law covers everyone in the country, including travellers in transit. You won't be driving on a layover, but public intoxication is also an offence, and people have been arrested at UAE airports after drinking heavily in the air — so ease up even on a stopover.
