Drinking in Bali: how to stay safe, spot methanol, and know the law

Bali is one of Australia's favourite getaways, and a cold Bintang by the pool is part of the holiday. But the island has a genuine, occasionally fatal risk that doesn't exist at home: methanol poisoning from bootleg spirits. Here's how to drink safely, count what you're actually drinking, and stay on the right side of the law.

The one thing to remember: the danger isn't beer or sealed, branded drinks — it's cheap, home-made or bootleg spirits and the cocktails made from them. The Australian Government warns that with methanol, "as little as one shot can be fatal." When in doubt, stick to sealed beer and wine.

The real danger: methanol poisoning

Methanol is a toxic form of alcohol. Unscrupulous producers sometimes add it to bulk out or strengthen cheap, informally-made spirits, and it can also form when home distilling is done badly. You can't see, smell or taste the difference in a drink — which is exactly what makes it so dangerous.

Which drinks carry the risk

Smartraveller, the Australian Government's travel advisory service, specifically warns travellers in Indonesia about methanol in:

  • Arak — a traditional local rice- or palm-based spirit, especially home-made or unbranded batches.
  • Locally-produced spirits and home-made alcoholic drinks of any kind.
  • Counterfeit branded bottles — fake "Smirnoff", "Absolut" and similar, refilled with cheap spirit.
  • Cheap cocktails, "free-flow" deals and spirit-based buckets, which may be mixed from any of the above.

Because high taxes make legitimate imported spirits expensive in Indonesia, suspiciously cheap spirits and bargain cocktail deals are exactly where the risk concentrates. Beer like Bintang, and sealed, branded wine, are not the problem.

Symptoms — and why the delay is so dangerous

At first, methanol poisoning can look just like being drunk — drowsiness, unsteadiness and a headache. The catch is that there is a latent period: the serious effects often don't appear for many hours (and the delay can be longer if you also drank ordinary alcohol). By the time the dangerous symptoms arrive, a lot of damage may already be done.

According to the World Health Organization, later symptoms of methanol poisoning include:

  • Headache, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain
  • Dizziness or vertigo and rapid, deep breathing (hyperventilation)
  • Visual problems — blurred vision, "snowfield" vision, or blindness
  • In severe cases, convulsions, coma and death from respiratory arrest
If you or a mate might have methanol poisoning, act immediately. Don't "sleep it off" — the delay is the trap. Get to a hospital straight away and say you suspect methanol poisoning; it's treated very differently from ordinary drunkenness (with antidotes such as ethanol or fomepizole, and sometimes dialysis), and early treatment saves sight and lives. Any unexplained vision problems after drinking are a red flag to seek urgent help.

How to protect yourself

  • Drink only branded, sealed drinks from reputable bars, restaurants and shops.
  • Be wary of spirits and cocktails, especially very cheap ones, "free-flow" or all-you-can-drink deals, and anything served from an unlabelled or refilled bottle.
  • Watch your drink being made from a sealed, branded bottle where you can.
  • Avoid home-made alcohol and local spirits like arak unless you are completely confident of the source.
  • If a drink tastes unusually strong, sharp or "off", stop drinking it.
  • Know where the nearest hospital is and keep your travel insurer's emergency number handy.

Don't forget drink spiking

Separate from methanol, drink spiking happens in party areas anywhere in the world, Bali included. Smartraveller's advice is simple: "Be alert, don't leave your food or drinks alone. Watch when your drink is being mixed." Stick with people you trust, look out for your mates, and if someone becomes unexpectedly and severely unwell, get medical help rather than assuming they're just drunk.

How much are you actually drinking?

Bintang goes down easy in the heat, and the large bottles are bigger than a standard Aussie serve — so it's easy to lose count. An Australian standard drink contains 10 g of pure alcohol, and you can work out any drink with volume (L) × %ABV × 0.789. Here's how common Bali drinks stack up:

Bodies work harder in the tropics. Heat and the sun dehydrate you, and alcohol does too, so a few drinks can hit harder than they would at home. Alternate every drink with water, eat properly, and don't drink in the midday heat on an empty stomach.

Alcohol and the law in Bali

A few things are worth knowing before you go:

  • The legal drinking age is 21. Alcohol can legally only be sold to people aged 21 and over across Indonesia, and venues can run spot checks.
  • Never drive or ride after drinking. Drink driving is illegal in Indonesia, and scooter crashes involving alcohol are tragically common — and frequently fatal. Your travel insurance will usually be void if you ride after drinking, or ride without a valid motorcycle licence, an International Driving Permit and a helmet. Use Grab, Gojek, a metered Bluebird taxi or a hotel/driver instead.
  • Drug laws are extremely severe. Penalties for drug offences in Indonesia are harsh and can include lengthy prison terms or the death penalty. Never accept, buy or carry anything you're unsure of — including things that might be slipped into a drink or sold alongside it.

A sensible plan for a big night in Bali

  1. Sort your transport first. Save Grab/Gojek on your phone, or agree on a driver, before you start drinking.
  2. Default to sealed beer or wine. If you want spirits, buy them at a reputable bar and watch the pour from a sealed, branded bottle.
  3. Skip the suspiciously cheap stuff. Bargain cocktails, "free-flow" deals and buckets are the worst value when the downside is methanol.
  4. Pace it — roughly one standard drink an hour, with water in between, and eat while you drink.
  5. Keep count. Large Bintangs and free-poured spirits add up fast; a quick tally keeps the night where you want it.
  6. Look after your group. Watch each other's drinks, and treat sudden severe illness — especially any vision problems — as a medical emergency, not just a big night.
A note on driving: whether you're on a scooter in Bali or back behind the wheel in Australia, no estimate or app can tell you that you're safe to drive. Your body still only clears alcohol at about one standard drink per hour, impairment starts well below any legal limit, and the safest blood alcohol level before driving or riding is zero. If you've been drinking, don't drive.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to drink alcohol in Bali?

Branded, sealed drinks like Bintang beer and bottled wine from reputable venues are generally fine. The real risk is methanol in home-made or bootleg spirits, cheap cocktails and counterfeit bottles — so be cautious with spirits and anything suspiciously cheap.

What is methanol and why is it so dangerous?

Methanol is a toxic alcohol sometimes added to cheap or home-made spirits. The Australian Government warns that as little as one shot can be fatal, symptoms are often delayed for hours, and they can progress to blindness, coma and death — which is why early hospital treatment is critical.

How many standard drinks is a Bintang?

Bintang is about 4.7% alcohol. A small 330ml bottle is roughly 1.2 Australian standard drinks; a large 620ml bottle is about 2.3.

Can I ride a scooter after a couple of drinks in Bali?

No. Drink driving is illegal, crashes are common and often fatal, and your travel insurance will usually be void. Use a ride-hailing app, taxi or driver.

Can I still be over the limit the next morning?

Yes. After a big night your body may still be clearing alcohol well into the next day, so plan your morning scooter ride or drive accordingly — or avoid it.
