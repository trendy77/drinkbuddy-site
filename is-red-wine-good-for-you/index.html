Is red wine good for you?

By Trendy Publishing editorial team · Published 16 October 2026 · Sources

For years red wine had a health halo — a daily glass was practically doctor's orders. That story is now falling apart. The resveratrol, the antioxidants and the famous "French paradox" don't hold up the way they were sold, and the world's major health bodies have quietly changed their advice. Here's what the evidence actually says in 2026.

The short answer

Red wine is not a health drink. It does contain antioxidants, and there's nothing wrong with enjoying a glass — but the idea that it actively improves your health doesn't survive a close look at the research. The compounds people point to are present in amounts far too small to help, and whatever tiny upside they might offer is outweighed by the alcohol they come packaged with. As the World Health Organization now puts it bluntly, when it comes to alcohol "there is no safe amount that does not affect health".

Where the "red wine is healthy" idea came from

Two things built the myth. The first was the French paradox — an observation from the 1980s that people in France seemed to have relatively low rates of heart disease despite a diet rich in saturated fat. Red wine got the credit. Later research pointed instead to a mix of other factors: differences in overall diet, lifestyle, portion sizes, and even the way heart-disease deaths were recorded at the time. The wine was never shown to be the cause.

The second was resveratrol, an antioxidant found in grape skins and therefore in red wine. In the lab, resveratrol looked promising for the heart, inflammation and ageing — and that's what filled the headlines. The catch is dose, which we'll get to below.

On top of those came a wave of population studies suggesting that light drinkers were healthier than people who didn't drink at all. For a while that "J-shaped curve" was treated as proof that a little alcohol was good for you. It's the part of the story that has unravelled the most.

Why the heart-health claim has fallen apart

The big problem with those older studies is who ended up in the "non-drinker" group. It often included people who had stopped drinking precisely because they were already ill — so the abstainers looked unhealthy not because they avoided alcohol, but because sick people had quit. When newer analyses correct for this "sick-quitter" effect and other confounders, the apparent benefit of light drinking shrinks dramatically or disappears.

The WHO is now direct about it: the possible protective effects suggested by some studies depend heavily on the comparison groups and statistical methods chosen, and there are no studies showing that any benefit for heart disease or diabetes outweighs the cancer risk that alcohol carries for the individual drinker. If protecting your heart is the goal, the US National Library of Medicine notes there are "much more effective ways to prevent heart disease" — managing your blood pressure and cholesterol, keeping a healthy weight, exercising and not smoking. None of them come with a cancer risk attached.

Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen. The International Agency for Research on Cancer puts alcohol in its highest-risk category — the same group as tobacco, asbestos and radiation. Alcohol causes at least seven types of cancer, and the WHO says the risk starts from the very first drop. That applies to the ethanol in red wine exactly as it does to any other drink. What about resveratrol and the antioxidants?

This is where the maths kills the myth. Red wine really does contain resveratrol and other polyphenol antioxidants, and in a test tube they can look anti-inflammatory and protective. But the doses used in those studies are enormous compared with what's in a glass. To get a meaningful amount of resveratrol from wine, you'd have to drink so much that — in the words of one dietitian at the MD Anderson Cancer Center — the alcohol intake would be "high enough that it would nullify a protective effect." In other words, the cure would do far more damage than the compound could ever undo.

There's also nothing special about the red. A 2025 meta-analysis found red wine offered no cancer-risk advantage over white wine, and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention states plainly that there is "no evidence that drinking red wine reduces the risk of cancer." If you want the polyphenols, the far better source is food: berries, grapes, nuts and dark chocolate deliver them with none of the alcohol.

So should you give up your glass of red?

Not necessarily — this isn't a lecture to quit. The honest message is simply that you should drink red wine because you enjoy it, not because you think it's doing your body a favour. If you like a glass with dinner, the sensible approach is to keep it genuinely moderate and know your numbers.

Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council advises healthy adults have no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day to keep health risks low. That's easier to blow through than it sounds: a standard 150 ml restaurant pour of red is about 1.5 standard drinks, so a "daily glass" quietly lands you around 10 a week — at the ceiling, not below it. A bigger 250 ml pour is closer to 2.5 standard drinks on its own.

It's not calorie-free, either. A standard glass of red carries roughly 125 calories, almost all from the alcohol itself — see calories in alcohol for how drinks compare. And because wine raises blood pressure with regular heavier intake, it works against the very heart health it was once sold on; more on that in does alcohol raise blood pressure?

The bottom line

The science has moved, and the verdict is clear: red wine is a pleasant drink, not a health supplement. There's no antioxidant, no resveratrol and no French-paradox magic that offsets what the alcohol does. If you drink it, drink it for the taste and the company — keep within the guidelines, match it with water and food, and if you'd like to drink less, here are evidence-based ways to cut down.

Frequently asked questions

Is red wine actually good for your heart?

The evidence that it protects your heart has weakened a lot. Older studies suggested light drinkers had less heart disease, but newer analyses show much of that was a statistical artefact — the "non-drinker" group often included people who had quit because they were already unwell. Health bodies now point to far safer, more effective ways to protect your heart: managing blood pressure and cholesterol, staying active and not smoking.

How much resveratrol is in red wine?

Not much. The doses of resveratrol that show effects in lab and animal studies are far higher than you could ever get from a glass — you'd have to drink so much that the alcohol would harm you long before the resveratrol helped. Dietitians recommend getting the same polyphenols from berries, grapes, nuts and dark chocolate instead.

Is red wine healthier than beer or white wine?

No meaningful difference for your health. A 2025 meta-analysis found red wine offered no cancer-risk advantage over white wine, and the CDC says there's no evidence red wine reduces cancer risk. The ethanol driving alcohol's risks is the same in every drink, so the total amount matters far more than the type.

Is one glass of red wine a day OK?

A daily glass is low-risk rather than no-risk. The WHO says no level of alcohol is completely safe, and a 150 ml glass of red is about 1.5 standard drinks — so a glass a day adds up to roughly 10 a week, right at the top of Australia's recommended limit rather than a health benefit.
