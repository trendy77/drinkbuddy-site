Alcohol blackouts: why you can't remember drinking

You woke up with a full night — or a chunk of it — simply missing. You weren't asleep. You were talking, walking, maybe even texting. So where did the memories go? The short answer: your brain stopped recording them.

A blackout is one of the more unsettling experiences a drinker can have, and it's more common than most people realise. It isn't a sign that your memory is broken, and it isn't "passing out". It's a specific, well-understood effect of a fast-rising blood alcohol level on the part of the brain that files away new memories. Here's what's actually happening, when it starts, and how to keep it from happening to you.

What a blackout actually is

An alcohol-induced blackout is a gap in your memory for events that happened while you were intoxicated. According to the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), these gaps happen when a person drinks enough that the brain temporarily can't move experiences from short-term storage into long-term storage — a process called memory consolidation.

The work happens in a brain region called the hippocampus. When alcohol floods the system quickly, it disrupts the hippocampus's ability to lay down new memories. The events still happen, and you experience them in the moment, but nothing is written to the "hard drive". That's why the memories can't be recalled later — they were never saved in the first place. Australia's healthdirect describes a blackout the same way: memory loss caused by drinking too much, too fast.

Blacking out is not passing out. Passing out means falling asleep or losing consciousness from drinking. During a blackout you're wide awake — chatting, ordering rounds, catching a taxi — but your brain isn't recording any of it. As your BAC keeps climbing, it's possible to slide from a blackout into passing out.

The two types of blackout

Blackouts come in two forms, defined by how much memory is lost.

Fragmentary blackouts (the common one)

Also called a "grey-out" or "brown-out", this is the more common type. You have spotty, partial memories — "islands" of recollection with blank stretches in between. Often a reminder from a friend the next day ("remember when you...") can trigger some of the missing pieces to surface.

En bloc blackouts (the total one)

This is complete amnesia, often covering hours at a stretch. With an en bloc blackout the memories genuinely never formed, so no cue or reminder will bring them back. As the NIAAA puts it, it's as if the events simply never happened.

When do blackouts start?

Blackouts are strongly tied to how high — and how fast — your blood alcohol concentration (BAC) rises. The NIAAA reports that blackouts tend to begin at a BAC of around 0.16 percent, which is more than three times Australia's full-licence driving limit of 0.05. At that level, most cognitive abilities — impulse control, attention, judgement and decision-making — are already heavily impaired, which is part of what makes blackout-level intoxication so risky.

But the number on its own doesn't tell the whole story. Published research shows blackouts are far more likely when alcohol enters the bloodstream quickly, spiking the BAC rather than raising it gradually. That's why the way you drink matters as much as how much.

Why women are more prone to blackouts

It isn't about tolerance or how "used to it" someone is. On average, women weigh less than men and, litre for litre, carry less water in their bodies. The same number of drinks therefore produces a higher peak BAC, and reaches it faster — exactly the fast-rising spike that drives blackouts. It's the same physiology that explains why alcohol affects women differently across the board, not a difference in willpower or experience.

Are blackouts a warning sign?

A single blackout isn't proof of an alcohol use disorder — they can happen to anyone who drinks enough, at any age and regardless of drinking experience. But the NIAAA is clear that even one blackout is a reason for concern and a prompt to think about your relationship with alcohol. Frequent blackouts, in particular, are strongly linked with other alcohol-related harms: injuries, ending up in emergency, missing work, and worse.

If blackouts are happening to you or someone you care about, it's worth a conversation with a GP or a confidential support service. In Australia you can call the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline on 1800 250 015 (free, 24/7), which connects you to your state or territory service.

How to lower the risk

Because blackouts are driven by a rapidly climbing BAC, the whole game is slowing that rise. None of this is complicated:

  • Eat first, and keep eating. Food in the stomach slows absorption and flattens the BAC spike. Read more on drinking on an empty stomach.
  • Pace to about one standard drink an hour. That's roughly the rate your body clears alcohol, so your BAC stays on a leash instead of spiking.
  • Alternate with water. One glass of water between drinks slows you down and keeps you hydrated.
  • Skip the shots and drinking games. They're engineered to raise BAC fast — the exact recipe for a blackout.
  • Never stack alcohol on sleep or anti-anxiety medication. The combination can trigger blackouts well below the usual threshold.
  • Keep count. Knowing your running tally is the simplest early-warning system there is.

Staying within the Australian guideline of no more than four standard drinks on any single day keeps you comfortably below blackout territory — the NHMRC's daily limit exists precisely to avoid the high, fast BAC spikes that cause harm.

A note on driving: a BAC anywhere near blackout levels is roughly three times or more Australia's 0.05 limit, and impairment starts well below that. Alcohol can also still be in your system the next morning. If you've been drinking, don't drive — and remember only time lowers BAC, never coffee, a shower or a big breakfast.

Frequently asked questions

What is an alcohol blackout?

A gap in memory for events that happened while intoxicated. The brain temporarily stops saving experiences to long-term memory, so those events are never stored. You're awake the whole time — it's a recording failure, not lost consciousness.

At what BAC do blackouts start?

The NIAAA says they tend to begin around a BAC of 0.16 — over three times the 0.05 full-licence limit — and higher. They can start lower when alcohol is mixed with sleep or anti-anxiety medication.

What's the difference between blacking out and passing out?

Blacking out is a memory failure while you're still awake and active. Passing out is falling asleep or losing consciousness. You can slide from one into the other as your BAC keeps rising.

Can you recover memories lost in a blackout?

Usually not. In a complete (en bloc) blackout the memories were never formed. In a partial (fragmentary) blackout, a reminder can sometimes trigger patchy recall.

How can I avoid blacking out?

Slow the BAC rise: eat, pace to about one drink an hour, alternate with water, avoid shots and drinking games, and keep count. Staying under four standard drinks a day keeps you well clear.
