Does alcohol kill brain cells?

It's one of the most repeated lines about drinking: every big night wipes out a batch of brain cells you'll never get back. It's also, strictly speaking, not true — but the real story is more interesting, and it's still a very good reason to keep an eye on how much you drink.

The short answer

Moderate, ordinary drinking does not kill the neurons in your brain outright. When scientists have counted the actual cells in the brains of people who drank heavily for years and compared them with non-drinkers, the total number of neurons in the neocortex — the main "thinking" layer of the brain — was not significantly lower.

So where did the myth come from, and why do doctors still take alcohol's effect on the brain seriously? Because killing whole cells isn't the only way to damage a brain. Alcohol goes after the connections between neurons instead, and that turns out to matter just as much.

Where the myth came from

The idea took hold because early research found that the brains of long-term heavy drinkers were visibly smaller and clearly damaged, and it was an easy leap to assume the missing volume meant dead neurons. Looking more closely, researchers found the shrinkage was mostly lost white matter — the insulated wiring that connects different brain regions — along with smaller, less branched neurons, rather than a wholesale cull of the cells themselves. The brain cells were still there; what had suffered was their ability to talk to each other.

What alcohol actually does to your brain

Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant, which means it slows down the signalling between neurons. That happens on two timescales.

Short term: scrambled signals

Everything you notice when someone is drunk is alcohol interfering with communication between brain cells — not cells dying. It dampens the cerebellum, the region that handles balance and coordination, which is why heavy drinking brings the stagger and the fumbled keys. It disrupts the prefrontal cortex, loosening judgement and self-control. And it interferes with the hippocampus, the brain's memory recorder.

That's what a blackout really is. When your blood alcohol climbs fast and high, the hippocampus stops laying down new memories. The night still happened, but it was never recorded, so there's nothing to recall the next day. It's a temporary failure to form memories — not proof that brain cells have been destroyed. We go deeper in our guide on alcohol blackouts.

These short-term effects lift as your body clears the alcohol, at roughly one standard drink an hour. The signalling returns to normal.

Long term: a shrinking brain

Repeated heavy drinking over years is a different matter. It can cause the brain to physically shrink — brain atrophy — as white matter is lost and neurons retract their branches, showing up as problems with memory, learning, attention and coordination. Because much of this damage is to the connections and wiring rather than the cells, some of it can be reversed when the drinking stops (more on that below).

The real brain-cell killer: Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome

There is one situation where heavy drinking genuinely does destroy brain tissue — but, revealingly, it's not the alcohol itself doing it directly. It's a vitamin deficiency.

Heavy drinkers often run short of thiamine (vitamin B1), because alcohol interferes with how the gut absorbs it and because a drinking-heavy diet tends to be a poor one. Thiamine is essential to brain cells, and a severe lack of it can cause Wernicke encephalopathy — a medical emergency marked by confusion, loss of coordination and abnormal eye movements. According to the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, if it isn't treated quickly it can progress to Korsakoff syndrome, which brings severe and often permanent memory loss and an inability to form new memories.

The good news is that Wernicke encephalopathy, caught early, is treated with thiamine — usually given straight into a vein — and some of the damage can be reversed. In Australia, bread-making flour is fortified with thiamine, a public-health measure that has helped reduce these cases.

This isn't medical advice. Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome is a serious condition. Sudden confusion, unsteadiness or vision changes in someone who drinks heavily should be treated as an emergency — call 000. If you're worried about your own or someone else's drinking, the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline is on 1800 250 015.

When alcohol really does kill brain cells

There are two situations where alcohol can kill neurons directly, and both involve a brain that is still being built.

The first is before birth. Alcohol crosses the placenta, and in a developing foetus it can kill neurons and disrupt how the brain wires itself — the basis of fetal alcohol spectrum disorder. This is why health authorities advise that no amount of alcohol is known to be safe in pregnancy.

The second is adolescence. The brain keeps developing until around age 25, and the Alcohol and Drug Foundation notes that because a young brain is still "developing, growing and rearranging", it is especially vulnerable to alcohol — which can cause lasting learning difficulties, memory problems and brain damage. Starting young also raises the risk of trouble down the track: people who begin drinking before 15 are about five times more likely to develop alcohol dependence later than those who start at 21.

What about "just a couple of drinks"?

If heavy drinking is the problem, is light drinking off the hook for your brain? The evidence is less comforting than it used to be. A 2022 study of more than 36,000 adults, published in the journal Nature Communications, found that even light-to-moderate drinking was linked to reductions in both grey and white matter — and the association began below a single daily drink.

The researchers put the effect in terms of brain ageing. For an average 50-year-old, going from one drink a day to two was associated with brain changes resembling about two extra years of ageing, and across the range up to four drinks a day the cumulative effect resembled more than a decade.

These are statistical associations across a large group, not a promise about any one person, and brain scans aren't the whole story. But the direction is clear, and it lines up with the World Health Organization's position that there is no safe level of alcohol for health. For the brain specifically, less is better and none is best.

Can your brain recover?

Here's the hopeful part. Because so much of alcohol's damage is to connections and white matter rather than to dead cells, the brain can repair a surprising amount of it once the drinking stops. Studies of people in recovery show brain volume rebuilding and thinking skills improving over weeks and months without alcohol — the brain is far more adaptable than the old myth ever allowed.

That recovery has limits: the memory loss of Korsakoff syndrome generally doesn't come back, and damage to a developing foetal brain is lasting. Which is the real takeaway — your brain can bounce back from a lot, but it's much easier to protect it than to repair it.

Drinking in a way that looks after your brain

You don't need to treat every drink as brain damage. But a few habits genuinely lower the load on your brain over time:

  • Stay within the national guidelines. Australia's advice is no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day — a ceiling, not a target.
  • Avoid the big, fast sessions. It's the high-and-fast blood alcohol spikes that hit the hippocampus and cause blackouts, so pace yourself at about one standard drink an hour and eat first.
  • Build in alcohol-free days to give your brain and body time to recover.
  • Don't lean on your tolerance. Feeling less drunk doesn't mean less harm — your tolerance changes how alcohol feels, not what it's doing to your brain.
  • Keep count. Most people underestimate how much they drink, and brain effects track the total amount over time.
A note on driving: none of this changes the rules behind the wheel. In Australia the legal limit for full-licence drivers is under 0.05, and zero for learner and provisional drivers. Your body clears only about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds that up — so if you've been drinking, don't drive. These figures are estimates and affect everyone differently.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol kill brain cells?

Not in the way the myth says. Counts of neurons in the brains of long-term heavy drinkers show the cells are largely still there; what alcohol damages are the connections between them — the dendrites and the white matter wiring. That still harms how the brain works, so it's no free pass.

Can your brain recover after you stop drinking?

Often yes, at least partly. Because the damage is largely to connections rather than dead cells, brain volume and thinking skills frequently improve over weeks and months of not drinking. Severe damage like Korsakoff syndrome can be permanent.

Do blackouts mean you've killed brain cells?

No. A blackout is the hippocampus failing to record new memories while your blood alcohol is high — the memories were never stored, not erased. Frequent blackouts are still a sign of risky drinking.

Is moderate drinking safe for your brain?

A 2022 study of over 36,000 adults linked even light-to-moderate drinking to reduced grey and white matter, starting below one drink a day. There's no established safe level of alcohol for brain health.

What is the most serious way alcohol damages the brain?

Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, caused by a thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency that's common in heavy drinkers. Wernicke encephalopathy is a medical emergency; untreated, it can progress to the permanent memory loss of Korsakoff syndrome.

Give your brain fewer big nights to recover from

The brain copes best when you avoid the fast, heavy sessions and keep within the guidelines — and that's far easier when you can see your count.
