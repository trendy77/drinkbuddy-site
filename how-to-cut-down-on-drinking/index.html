How to cut down on drinking

Cutting down doesn't have to mean giving up. A few small, deliberate habits — knowing your limit, pacing yourself, and building in alcohol-free days — can take the edge off your weekly total without taking the fun out of a night out.

Most people who want to drink a bit less aren't looking to quit. They just want to wake up clearer, spend less, sleep better, and feel more in control of the tab. The good news is that the changes that work best are simple and practical — not willpower marathons. Here's an evidence-based plan you can start this week.

First, know what "too much" actually looks like

It's hard to cut down when you don't know your starting point. In Australia, the NHMRC guidelines recommend that healthy adults have no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 standard drinks on any one day. That's a ceiling for keeping risk low — not a target to aim for.

The catch is that a "standard drink" is smaller than most people pour. One standard drink is 10 grams of alcohol — but an average restaurant glass of wine is about 1.6 standard drinks, a full-strength pub beer is around 1.6, and a 375 mL can of pre-mixed spirits is about 1.5. Three generous glasses of wine at dinner can quietly become five standard drinks. If you've never counted, that's step one.

The awareness effect. People consistently underestimate how much they drink. Simply counting your standard drinks — writing each one down or logging it in an app — tends to bring the total down on its own, because you can no longer lose track mid-round.

In-the-moment tips: drink less on the night

These are the changes that healthdirect and the Alcohol and Drug Foundation point to as the most effective on any given occasion:

  • Set a limit before you start — decide how many drinks you'll have before the first one, and stick to it. It's far easier to commit while sober.
  • Count every standard drink — not glasses or cans, standard drinks. A pint or a cocktail can be two in one.
  • Alternate with water — one glass of water (or a soft drink) between each alcoholic drink slows your pace and keeps you hydrated.
  • Order smaller serves — a pot instead of a pint, a single instead of a double, a small wine instead of a large.
  • Sip, don't gulp — put your glass down between sips, and finish a drink before you order the next.
  • Skip the rounds — buying in rounds forces your pace to match the fastest drinker in the group. Buy your own.
  • Don't let anyone top you up — a constantly refilled glass is impossible to count. Let it empty first.
  • Choose lower-strength drinks — mid-strength beer, a spritz, or a lighter wine gets you the same social ritual with less alcohol.
  • Eat before and while you drink — food slows absorption and slows your pace. Skip the salty snacks that just make you thirstier.
  • Keep your hands and mind busy — pool, darts, dancing or a good conversation all naturally slow how fast you drink.

Longer-term tips: change the pattern, not just the night

Drinking less over the long run is really about habits. A few structural changes make it stick:

  • Plan two or more alcohol-free days a week. Healthdirect specifically recommends this. Regular dry days break the reflex of pouring a drink out of routine rather than genuine want.
  • Know your triggers. Notice when and where you tend to drink — the Friday wind-down, stress, boredom, certain mates. Once you can name the trigger, you can plan an alternative for it.
  • Line up alcohol-free options. Organise catch-ups that aren't built around a bar — coffee, a walk, a meal at a venue with good mocktails or zero-alcohol beers.
  • Don't keep a big stockpile at home. If it's not in the fridge, having "just one" takes more effort — and that friction helps.
  • Track your progress. Watching your weekly total drop is genuinely motivating, and it turns a vague goal into something you can see.

Why it's worth it

Cutting back pays off faster than people expect. Drinking less is linked to better sleep, steadier mood and less anxiety, more money in your pocket, and a lower long-term risk of serious conditions including liver disease, heart disease, several cancers and stroke. Even trimming from the top of your weekly total lowers your risk — the benefit is dose-dependent, so every drink you skip counts.

Cutting down and driving are separate questions. Drinking less is great for your health, but it doesn't change how fast alcohol leaves your body — that's still about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds it up. If you've been drinking, don't drive. In Australia the limit is under 0.05 for full-licence drivers and zero for learners and P-platers.

When to get a hand

If you find you can't cut down even when you want to, if you feel you need a drink to get going, or if you drink heavily every day, it's worth talking to your doctor. Stopping suddenly after heavy, regular drinking can actually be dangerous, so a GP can help you reduce safely and point you to support. You can also call the healthdirect helpline on 1800 022 222 (24/7), or try the free Daybreak app from Hello Sunday Morning. Asking for help early is a sign of good sense, not failure.

Frequently asked questions

How much is it safe to drink?

No amount is completely safe, but to keep the risk low the NHMRC recommends no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day for healthy adults. A standard drink is 10 grams of alcohol.

What's the easiest way to cut down?

Set a limit before you go out, count each standard drink, and alternate every alcoholic drink with water. Those three habits do most of the heavy lifting.

How many alcohol-free days should I have?

Aim for two or more each week. It breaks the automatic-pour habit and gives your body time to recover.

When should I see a doctor about my drinking?

If you can't cut down when you want to, need a drink to function, or drink heavily every day. A doctor can help you reduce safely, since stopping suddenly after heavy drinking can be risky.
