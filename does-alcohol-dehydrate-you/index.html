Does alcohol dehydrate you?

Short answer: yes. Alcohol is a diuretic — it makes your kidneys shed more water than you take in, which is why you're up and down to the loo all night and wake up parched. Here's the science, roughly how much fluid you lose, and simple ways to stay ahead of it.

Why alcohol makes you pee more

The culprit is a hormone called vasopressin — also known as antidiuretic hormone, or ADH. Its job is to tell your kidneys how much water to hold on to. When you're well hydrated, vasopressin drops and you pass more urine; when you're low on fluid, it rises and your kidneys reabsorb water to conserve it.

Alcohol interferes with this system. It suppresses the release of vasopressin from the pituitary gland, so your kidneys stop reabsorbing as much water and send more of it straight to your bladder. The result is the familiar "breaking the seal" effect — once you start, the trips to the toilet keep coming. As Drinkaware explains, that extra urine output is the reason you can end the night having lost more fluid than you drank.

In plain terms: alcohol switches off the hormone that normally tells your body to keep water. Your kidneys respond by flushing fluid out faster than you're replacing it — and dehydration creeps in.

How much fluid do you actually lose?

Research on the diuretic effect of alcohol suggests that each gram of alcohol can add up to about 10 ml of urine output. One Australian standard drink contains 10 g of pure alcohol, so as a rough rule of thumb that's around 100 ml of extra urine per standard drink — over and above the fluid in the drink itself.

Two important caveats keep this honest. First, the effect is strongest early on. A study in Alcohol and Alcoholism found that when people were already well hydrated, a small dose of alcohol produced about 158 ml of extra urine over four hours — but when they started out slightly dehydrated, the same dose produced only about 87 ml. Once your body senses it's short on water, vasopressin fights back and the diuretic effect fades. Second, the effect is front-loaded and temporary: it's most pronounced while your blood alcohol is rising, not for the rest of the day.

None of that makes alcohol a hydrating drink — it just means the "you lose four glasses for every one you drink" line you may have heard is an overstatement. You do lose net fluid; you don't drain yourself dry with a single beer.

Not all drinks are equal

How dehydrating a drink is depends on two things: how much alcohol it contains, and how much water comes with it.

A schooner of beer delivers a lot of water alongside the alcohol, which partly offsets the diuretic effect. A neat spirit gives you the diuretic with almost no water to go with it. That's why a night on spirits tends to leave you thirstier than the same amount of alcohol taken as beer — though the total alcohol is what matters most for both dehydration and your blood alcohol level.

Dehydration and the hangover

It's tempting to blame the entire next-day hangover on dehydration, but that's only part of the picture. Dehydration explains the thirst, dry mouth, headache and light-headedness — but the nausea, fatigue and general awfulness come from other processes too: the toxic breakdown product acetaldehyde, an inflammatory immune response, disrupted sleep, low blood sugar, and congeners (the flavour compounds found in higher amounts in darker drinks like whisky and red wine).

This is why chugging a litre of water before bed helps but doesn't grant you a hangover-free morning. Rehydrating tackles one contributor; the rest still need time. For a fuller breakdown, see our guide on how long a hangover lasts.

Watch the heat. Drinking in the sun — at the footy, a festival or a backyard barbecue — stacks a diuretic on top of sweating. That combination dehydrates you far faster than drinking indoors, and it's a common reason people feel unwell at outdoor events. Water and shade matter more than usual on a hot day.

How to stay hydrated when you drink

You can't cancel out alcohol's diuretic effect, but you can stay well ahead of the fluid loss. The healthdirect advice is simple and effective:

  • Start hydrated. Have a glass of water before your first drink, especially on a hot day or after exercise.
  • Alternate one-for-one. A glass of water between each alcoholic drink replaces fluid, slows your pace and cuts your total intake — a triple win.
  • Choose longer, weaker drinks. Mid-strength beer or a spritzer topped with soda water gives you more fluid per standard drink than a neat spirit.
  • Rehydrate before bed. A decent glass or two of water before you turn in helps you start the morning less depleted.
  • Eat while you drink. Food slows absorption and helps steady blood sugar — see why an empty stomach makes everything worse.

Sports drinks and electrolyte sachets can help if you've lost a lot of fluid, but plain water does most of the job for most people. And no amount of water lowers your blood alcohol content or sobers you up — hydration eases dehydration, nothing more.

Staying hydrated is not the same as being sober. Drinking water alongside alcohol does not reduce your blood alcohol content and does not make you safe to drive. Only time lowers BAC. If you've been drinking, don't get behind the wheel — including the morning after.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol dehydrate you?

Yes. It suppresses vasopressin (ADH), so your kidneys hold less water and you pass more urine. Without replacing that fluid you become dehydrated, which feeds into hangover symptoms.

How much water do you lose per drink?

Roughly 100 ml of extra urine per standard drink as a rule of thumb (about 10 ml per gram of alcohol), with the biggest effect early in the night and when you begin well hydrated.

Does drinking water between alcoholic drinks help?

Yes — it replaces fluid, slows your pace and lowers your total intake. It won't reduce your BAC or make you safe to drive, but it eases dehydration.

Is beer more hydrating than spirits?

Beer carries more water and less alcohol per serve, so it offsets some of the loss. But it's still an alcoholic diuretic, not a hydration drink — water is best for rehydrating.

Does dehydration cause hangovers?

It's one cause among several. Acetaldehyde, inflammation, poor sleep and congeners all play a part, so rehydrating helps but won't prevent a hangover by itself.
