Alcohol and anxiety: why the day after feels so bad

A few drinks can feel like they melt stress away. Then you wake at 3am with your heart racing and a wave of dread you can't explain. That's hangxiety — and there's a clear brain-chemistry reason it happens.

Alcohol and anxiety have a two-faced relationship. In the moment, alcohol genuinely calms you. The next morning, it can leave you more anxious than before you started. Understanding why makes the pattern much easier to break — or at least to see coming.

Why alcohol feels calming at first

Alcohol is a depressant, which means it slows down the signals travelling between your brain and body. It does this mainly by boosting a calming brain chemical called GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) and dampening glutamate, the chemical that keeps your nervous system alert and excited.

More GABA and less glutamate is a recipe for feeling loose, relaxed and less inhibited. That's the pleasant, tension-melting effect people chase after a stressful day. The problem is that your brain doesn't like being pushed off balance, so it starts pushing back.

The rebound: where hangxiety comes from

As you keep drinking, your brain tries to restore equilibrium by turning down its own GABA and ramping up glutamate to counter the alcohol. This works fine while the alcohol is still in your system. But once you stop drinking and the alcohol clears, that counter-adjustment is left exposed: you're now low on the calming chemical and high on the excitatory one.

The result is a nervous system stuck in overdrive — often described as neurochemical whiplash. You feel wired, jittery, restless and anxious, sometimes with a racing heart or a sense of dread that has no obvious cause. This rebound tends to hit hardest in the hours after alcohol has fully left your body, which is why hangxiety so often peaks the morning after, or even the following afternoon.

In short: alcohol borrows calm from tomorrow. The relaxation you feel at night is repaid, with interest, as anxiety the next day.

It's not just brain chemistry

The GABA-glutamate rebound is the engine, but several other next-day effects pour fuel on the fire:

FactorHow it feeds anxiety
Broken sleepAlcohol wrecks the deep, restorative stages of sleep and causes early waking. Poor sleep alone makes anyone more anxious and less able to cope.
DehydrationAlcohol is a diuretic, so you lose fluid. Mild dehydration can bring headaches and a foggy, on-edge feeling.
Low blood sugarDrinking can drop your blood glucose, which can leave you shaky, irritable and anxious.
Raised stress hormonesA hangover is a mild stress state, with cortisol and adrenaline elevated — the same chemistry as feeling anxious.
Social worryFoggy memories of the night can trigger embarrassment and rumination — "what did I say?" — that layers on top of the physical dread.

The self-medicating trap

Because alcohol relieves anxiety in the short term, it's tempting to use it as a coping tool — a wine to take the edge off, a beer to steady the nerves. The catch is that the rebound leaves you more anxious than baseline, so it's easy to reach for another drink to smooth that out too.

Over time, regular heavy drinking can deplete the brain chemicals that help you manage stress, meaning everyday worries feel harder to handle without a drink. This is how a short-term calmer can quietly become a long-term driver of anxiety, and in some cases dependence. If you find you're drinking mainly to manage how you feel, that's a signal worth paying attention to — and a good reason to talk to your GP.

People with anxiety are especially affected. If you already live with anxiety, the rebound can feel sharper and the temptation to self-medicate is stronger. Alcohol can also interfere with anti-anxiety and antidepressant medications. It's always worth checking with your doctor or pharmacist about how alcohol interacts with anything you're taking.

How to reduce hangxiety

You can't cheat the chemistry, but you can blunt it. The single most effective step is simply drinking less, because a smaller push off balance means a smaller rebound. Practical habits that help:

  • Pace yourself. Keep to about one standard drink an hour so your system never swings too far.
  • Alternate with water. A glass of water between drinks slows you down and eases dehydration.
  • Eat first. Food before and during drinking slows absorption and steadies blood sugar.
  • Set a number before you start. Deciding in advance is far easier than deciding at the bar.
  • Protect your sleep. Finish drinking a few hours before bed so alcohol interferes less with your rest.
  • Stay within the guidelines. Australia's guidelines recommend no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 in a single day to reduce the risk of harm.

If hangxiety does hit, the reassuring news is that it's temporary. Hydrate, eat something, get outside for a walk, and remind yourself that the dread is a chemical rebound that will pass as your brain rebalances — usually within a day.

When to seek help

Occasional next-day nerves after a big night are common. But if anxiety is lingering for days, keeps returning, or you're relying on alcohol to feel calm, it's worth reaching out. Your GP is a good first step. In Australia you can also contact the free, confidential healthdirect service on 1800 022 222, the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline on 1800 250 015, or Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636 for mental health support. If you're ever in crisis, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or 000 in an emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Why does alcohol make my anxiety worse the next day?

Alcohol boosts the calming chemical GABA and dampens the excitatory chemical glutamate. As it clears, your brain overcorrects — GABA drops and glutamate surges — leaving your nervous system over-excited. You feel that as anxiety, restlessness and dread, often called hangxiety.

How long does hangxiety last?

For most people it eases within a day as brain chemistry rebalances, often peaking once alcohol has fully left your system. Poor sleep, dehydration and low blood sugar can stretch it out. If it lingers for several days, see your GP.

Does drinking help anxiety?

Only briefly, and it backfires. The short-lived calm is followed by a rebound that usually makes anxiety worse, and leaning on alcohol to cope can build a cycle that raises the risk of dependence.

How can I reduce hangxiety?

Drink less and more slowly, alternate with water, eat before and while you drink, protect your sleep, and stay within the Australian guidelines. Tracking your drinks helps you see the link between how much you drink and how you feel the next day.
