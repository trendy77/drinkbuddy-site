Does alcohol affect you more on a plane?

Short answer: altitude doesn't put more alcohol in your blood — a wine at 35,000 feet gives you the same reading as one on the ground. But the cabin environment can make it feel stronger, and new research shows drinking then sleeping mid-flight is harder on your body than most people realise.

The myth: "one drink in the air equals two on the ground"

It's one of the most repeated bits of travel folklore, and it traces back to a thinly evidenced study from the 1930s. The idea stuck because it feels true — but when researchers actually measured it, the numbers didn't back it up.

The United States Federal Aviation Administration tested it in 1970 and found no measurable link between altitude and how strongly alcohol registered in the blood. A later experiment comparing people drinking at 10,000 feet with people drinking at sea level produced near-identical blood alcohol concentration (BAC) results. The reason is simple: the liver enzyme that breaks down alcohol works at the same pace regardless of cabin pressure. Your BAC rises and falls just as it does on the ground — roughly one standard drink cleared per hour.

The bottom line on BAC: two drinks on a plane leave you with the same blood alcohol level as two drinks in your lounge room. Altitude does not multiply the alcohol. What changes is how you experience it.

Why drinks genuinely feel stronger up there

Even though your BAC is unchanged, plenty of people honestly feel woozier after a couple of in-flight drinks. That's not imagination — it's the cabin environment stacking several effects on top of the alcohol.

Less oxygen in the cabin

Aircraft cabins aren't pressurised to sea level. At cruising altitude the cabin sits at the equivalent of roughly 1,800 to 2,400 metres of elevation, and your blood oxygen saturation naturally drifts down towards 90% in a healthy adult. This mild oxygen shortage — hypoxia — can cause light-headedness, tiredness, slower thinking and reduced coordination. Those symptoms overlap almost perfectly with feeling tipsy, so the two sensations blur together.

Dry air and dehydration

Cabin humidity often sits around 10–20%, far drier than a typical room. You lose water with every breath, and alcohol is a diuretic that makes your kidneys shed even more. Dehydration won't raise your BAC, but it can leave you feeling foggy, headachey and worse for wear — and it sets you up for a rougher landing. If you want the detail, see our guide on whether alcohol dehydrates you.

Fatigue, jet lag and a strange environment

Early starts, disrupted body clocks, cramped seats and the general disorientation of travel all lower your tolerance for feeling off. Add a drink or two and it's easy to feel more affected than the same drinks would leave you at home.

What a 2024 study found about drinking and sleeping in flight

The most useful recent evidence comes from researchers at the German Aerospace Center, published in the journal Thorax in 2024. They took 48 healthy people aged 18 to 40 and had them sleep across two nights — some at normal air pressure, some at a simulated cabin pressure equivalent to about 2,400 metres. Before one of the nights, participants drank the equivalent of two beers or two glasses of wine; the other night they stayed sober.

The combination of alcohol and cabin pressure hit their bodies harder than either factor alone:

During sleepCabin pressure, no alcoholCabin pressure + alcohol
Median blood oxygen (SpO₂)~88%~85%
Median heart rate~73 bpm~88 bpm
Deep sleepHigherReduced

In other words, drinking before sleeping at altitude dropped blood oxygen lower and kept the heart working faster for a prolonged stretch — even in young, healthy people. The researchers noted the effects could be more pronounced in older travellers and anyone with existing heart or lung conditions, and suggested it may be worth rethinking easy access to alcohol on long-haul flights. Alcohol also suppresses restorative deep sleep, which is why an in-flight nightcap tends to leave you groggier, not more rested — the same pattern we cover in our guide to alcohol and sleep.

Worth knowing: the drop to around 85% blood oxygen during sleep is well below what's typical awake at sea level. For most healthy travellers on a short flight it's not dangerous, but combining alcohol with sleep on a long-haul flight — especially if you have a heart or lung condition — is a reasonable thing to be cautious about.

Drinking on a flight without overdoing it

None of this means you can't enjoy a drink on holiday. A few simple habits keep it in check:

  • Alternate with water. One glass of water for every alcoholic drink counters the dry-cabin dehydration.
  • Eat something. Drinking on an empty stomach at the start of a long flight is a fast track to feeling awful.
  • Keep count. It's easy to lose track when the crew keeps topping you up and there's no last-drinks bell. Count your standard drinks.
  • Don't drink right before sleeping on a long-haul flight — that's exactly the combination the research flagged.
  • Go easy before you land. If you're driving after the flight, your body still only clears about one standard drink per hour. See how long until your BAC is zero to plan the gap.
A note on driving after you land: a big travel day plus a few drinks can leave you over the limit long after touchdown. In Australia the limit is under 0.05 for full-licence holders and zero for learner and provisional drivers. Feeling sober is not the same as being under the limit — and estimates are only estimates. If you've been drinking, don't drive.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol raise your blood alcohol level more at altitude?

No. Your BAC for a given number of drinks is the same in the air as on the ground, and your liver clears it at the same rate — about one standard drink per hour.

Why do drinks feel stronger on a plane?

Lower cabin oxygen, dry air, dehydration, fatigue and jet lag all produce sensations that overlap with feeling drunk, even though your actual BAC hasn't changed.

Is it bad to drink and then sleep on a long flight?

A 2024 study found it lowered blood oxygen and raised heart rate during sleep even in healthy young adults, and cut deep sleep. It's more of a concern for older travellers and those with heart or lung conditions.

How can I drink more sensibly in flight?

Alternate with water, eat, keep count of your standard drinks, skip the pre-sleep nightcap, and leave time to sober up before driving.
