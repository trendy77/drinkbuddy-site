Alcohol and exercise: does it kill your gains?

You trained hard, then had a few beers to celebrate. The bad news: alcohol can cut your post-workout muscle repair by up to a third, dehydrate you and wreck the sleep your body needs to recover. Here's what the science actually says — and how to train hard without undoing your progress.

The short answer

Alcohol and exercise pull in opposite directions. Training is a signal for your body to repair, adapt and grow stronger. Alcohol interferes with almost every part of that recovery process — how your muscles rebuild, how well you rehydrate, how deeply you sleep, and how you fuel up afterwards. A single big session won't erase months of work, but regular heavy drinking around training makes your effort in the gym far less effective.

The headline finding: in a study of resistance-trained men, drinking a large dose of alcohol after a workout reduced the rate of muscle protein synthesis by 24% even when protein was consumed alongside it — and by 37% when the alcohol was taken with carbohydrate instead.

How alcohol blunts muscle recovery

After resistance training, your muscles ramp up muscle protein synthesis — the process of repairing tiny fibres and building them back stronger. This is where training adaptations actually happen, and it's driven by a signalling pathway called mTOR.

A well-known 2014 study published in PLOS ONE put this to the test. Eight physically active men completed a demanding workout — weights followed by interval cycling — then consumed protein, protein plus alcohol, or carbohydrate plus alcohol. The alcohol dose was large, around 12 standard drinks spread over the recovery period. Even when protein was on board, alcohol knocked muscle protein synthesis down by roughly a quarter compared with protein alone. In other words, alcohol suppressed the exact anabolic response the workout was supposed to trigger.

Protein offered partial protection, but it didn't cancel the effect. Alcohol also nudges up inflammation and disrupts the hormonal environment your muscles rely on to rebuild, which is part of why the Australian Alcohol and Drug Foundation notes that heavy drinking can negatively alter immune function, blood flow and protein synthesis after exercise.

Dehydration works against you

Exercise already costs you fluid through sweat. Alcohol makes it worse: it's a diuretic, meaning it suppresses the hormone that tells your kidneys to hold onto water, so you pee out more than you take in. Turning up to train already dehydrated — or drinking heavily straight after a session without rehydrating — leaves you more prone to cramps, reduced strength and slower recovery.

If you exercise in the heat, the effect compounds: sweat plus a diuretic is a fast route to feeling flat and headachy. Water between drinks and a proper rehydrate before bed both help, but they don't reverse the other effects of alcohol on recovery.

Sleep: where recovery really happens

Much of your muscle repair and growth-hormone release happens during deep sleep. Alcohol is deceptive here — it helps you fall asleep faster, but it suppresses restorative REM sleep and fragments the second half of the night. You wake feeling unrested, which blunts recovery and saps motivation for the next day's session. If you train seriously, protecting your sleep is one of the strongest arguments for keeping alcohol in check.

Alcohol, performance and injury

Drinking the night before affects more than how you feel. Dehydration, disrupted sleep and lingering fatigue reduce endurance, strength, reaction time and coordination the next day. That combination doesn't just lower performance — it raises the risk of strains, missteps and injury, especially in sports that demand quick, precise movement.

The kilojoule problem

If your goal is fat loss or body composition, alcohol quietly works against you on two fronts. First, it's energy-dense: about 29 kilojoules per gram, second only to fat, with almost no useful nutrition. Second, while your body is busy clearing alcohol it treats it as a priority fuel and pushes fat-burning to the back of the queue. Add the late-night snacking that often comes with a few drinks, and a hard week of training can be undone by a big weekend.

How to train hard and still enjoy a drink

You don't have to be teetotal to make progress. The goal is to reduce the overlap between drinking and the windows when your body is trying to recover.

  • Recover first, drink later. After a session, rehydrate with water and eat a meal with protein and carbohydrate before any alcohol. Give recovery a head start.
  • Keep it light. The research used a big dose — around 12 standard drinks. A couple of drinks does far less than a heavy session. Fewer drinks, less damage.
  • Match every drink with water. A one-for-one water rule blunts the dehydration and helps you sleep less badly.
  • Protect the night before a big effort. If you've got a game, a long run or a heavy lift tomorrow, skip drinking tonight — that's when it costs you most.
  • Use alcohol-free days. Spacing out your drinking gives your body clear windows to adapt, and helps you stay under the national guidelines.
The guideline to remember: Australia's NHMRC guidelines recommend healthy adults have no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day, to reduce the risk of alcohol-related harm. Training doesn't earn you extra — if anything, it's a reason to stay well under.
Never train — or drive — with alcohol still in your system. Exercising while intoxicated is dangerous: your balance, judgement and coordination are impaired, and dehydration is already working against you. And no workout, sauna or sweat session lowers your BAC any faster — only time does that.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol affect muscle growth?

Yes. After a workout, a large dose of alcohol reduced muscle protein synthesis by around 24% even alongside protein, and 37% with carbohydrate. Alcohol suppresses the anabolic signalling your muscles use to repair and grow.

How long after a workout should I wait before drinking?

There's no proven safe window, but more time is better. Rehydrate, eat a proper meal and let recovery begin first. Lighter drinking does less damage than a heavy session.

Can I drink and still lose weight or get fit?

It's harder but not impossible. Alcohol adds empty kilojoules, pauses fat-burning and disrupts sleep and motivation. Cutting back and keeping alcohol-free days makes your goals much more achievable.

Is it OK to exercise with a hangover?

Light movement is usually fine, but you'll be dehydrated, under-fuelled and poorly rested, so keep intensity low and rehydrate first. Never exercise if alcohol may still be in your system.
