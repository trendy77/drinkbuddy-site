Why does alcohol make my face red?

That warm, red glow after a couple of drinks has a name — the alcohol flush reaction. It looks harmless, even endearing, but it's actually your body waving a small red flag. Here's what's really going on.

For some people, half a glass of wine is enough to turn the face, neck and ears bright pink. Others can drink all night and stay their normal colour. If you're in the first group, you've probably been teased about it, told to "slow down", or handed a glass of water. But the flush isn't about drinking too fast or being a lightweight — it's about a specific step in how your body breaks alcohol down, and it's largely written into your genes.

The two-step clean-up your body does with alcohol

When you have a drink, your body treats the alcohol (ethanol) as a toxin and gets to work removing it. That happens in two main steps, mostly in the liver:

  1. An enzyme called alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) converts ethanol into a substance called acetaldehyde.
  2. A second enzyme, aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2), converts that acetaldehyde into acetate — a harmless compound your body then breaks down to water and carbon dioxide.

Acetaldehyde is the troublemaker in the middle. It's far more toxic than the alcohol itself, and it's the chemical behind a lot of what makes drinking feel bad. In a body with fully working enzymes, acetaldehyde is cleared so quickly you never notice it. The problem starts when step two runs slowly.

What the red face actually is

People who flush have an ALDH2 that doesn't work properly. So the acetaldehyde from step one gets made as usual, but the clean-up in step two lags behind. Acetaldehyde piles up in the bloodstream — and one of the things it does is make small blood vessels widen. Those dilated vessels near the surface of the skin are what you see as flushing: a pronounced reddening of the face and neck, sometimes spreading across the whole body.

The redness rarely travels alone. A build-up of acetaldehyde also tends to bring a racing or pounding heartbeat, a hot or prickly feeling, headache, and sometimes nausea. It's the same family of effects that certain "don't drink on this" medications deliberately produce — they work by blocking ALDH2 on purpose to make drinking unpleasant.

It's genetic, not a habit. The flush is usually caused by an inherited variant of the ALDH2 gene. You're born with it or you're not — it isn't something you build up over years of drinking, and you can't train it away. Drinking more often won't "toughen you up" out of it.

Why it's called "Asian flush"

You'll often hear the flush nicknamed "Asian flush" or "Asian glow". That's because the inactive ALDH2 gene variant is far more common in people of East Asian descent. Estimates suggest it affects roughly 35 to 40 per cent of East Asian people — those of Chinese, Japanese and Korean ancestry in particular — and around 540 million people worldwide, or about 8 per cent of the global population. It's much rarer in people of European or sub-Saharan African descent.

Not everyone flushes to the same degree. Some people go slightly pink; others turn deep red after a single drink and feel genuinely unwell. The strength of the reaction depends partly on whether you inherited the variant gene from one parent or both.

The part worth taking seriously

Here's why the flush is more than a cosmetic quirk. Acetaldehyde — the very thing building up when you flush — is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the World Health Organization's cancer agency, the same top-tier category as tobacco. If your body is slow to clear acetaldehyde, drinking exposes your tissues to more of it, for longer.

That has real consequences. A widely cited study in PLOS Medicine flagged the flushing response as an unrecognised risk factor for oesophageal cancer, reporting that people with the ALDH2 deficiency who drink moderately to heavily can face something like 6 to 10 times the risk of oesophageal (squamous cell) cancer compared with drinkers who clear acetaldehyde normally. The researchers made a striking point: the flush is perhaps the only clearly visible outward sign that a person carries a heightened, alcohol-related cancer risk.

The takeaway isn't panic — it's information. If you flush, your body is showing you, in real time, that it doesn't handle alcohol well. The single most effective response is to drink less, or not at all. To dig into the wider picture, see our guide on how alcohol is linked to cancer.

Please don't just cover it up

Because the redness can feel embarrassing, some people reach for antihistamines or heartburn tablets (H2 blockers) to hide it, and there are products marketed for exactly this. It's an understandable urge — but it's the wrong fix, for a few reasons.

Blocking the redness does nothing about the acetaldehyde still accumulating underneath, so the health risk carries on regardless. Worse, hiding the flush removes the very cue that would normally get you to slow down, which can lead people to drink more than they otherwise would. And mixing antihistamines with alcohol stacks up drowsiness and slows reactions. Toxicologists put it plainly: the flush is the body saying it's had enough, and covering it with medication just mutes a warning without removing the danger.

A true allergy is different. The everyday flush is an enzyme issue, not an immune reaction. But genuine alcohol intolerance or allergy — bringing hives, swelling, wheezing or a blocked nose — does exist and is rare. If drinking triggers those symptoms, or if flushing comes with severe headaches or a very fast heartbeat, it's worth seeing your GP.

If you flush, what can you actually do?

There's no way to fix a slow ALDH2 enzyme, so the sensible moves are all about reducing how much acetaldehyde your body has to deal with:

  • Drink less, or skip it. Fewer drinks means less acetaldehyde and less flushing — and it's the only genuine way to lower the associated risk.
  • Slow the pace. Around one standard drink an hour gives your enzymes more time to keep up. Alternate with water and don't drink on an empty stomach.
  • Skip the "flush blockers". Antihistamines and heartburn tablets hide the sign, not the cause.
  • Know your numbers. To keep health risks low, the Australian guidelines recommend no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day for healthy adults. If you flush, treat those as a firm ceiling, not a target.

For more everyday tactics, the government's healthdirect tips for safe drinking and the NHMRC alcohol guidelines are solid starting points, and our guide on how to cut down on drinking has practical steps.

A note on driving: flushing tells you nothing useful about whether you're under the limit. Whether or not your face goes red, the legal limit for full-licence drivers in Australia is under 0.05%, and zero for learner and probationary drivers. Only time lowers your blood alcohol — roughly one standard drink an hour — so if you've been drinking, don't drive. Any estimate is an estimate, never a green light.

Frequently asked questions

Why does alcohol make my face red?

Your body turns alcohol into a toxic by-product called acetaldehyde, then clears it with an enzyme called ALDH2. If your ALDH2 works poorly — usually an inherited trait — acetaldehyde builds up, your blood vessels widen, and your face and neck flush red, often with a fast heartbeat, warmth and nausea.

Is a red face when drinking dangerous?

The flush itself isn't harmful in the moment, but it means acetaldehyde, a Group 1 carcinogen, is building up. People with the flushing gene who drink regularly have a much higher risk of oesophageal cancer, so it's best read as a warning to drink less.

What is Asian flush and who gets it?

It's the common name for the alcohol flush reaction, so called because the ALDH2 gene variant behind it is far more common in people of East Asian descent — affecting around 35 to 40 per cent of East Asians and roughly 540 million people worldwide. It's inherited, not something you develop.

Can I stop the alcohol flush with antihistamines?

They can reduce the redness, but only hide the visible sign — the acetaldehyde and its risks remain, masking the flush can lead you to drink more, and antihistamines plus alcohol add extra drowsiness. Doctors advise against covering it up with medication.

Does flushing mean I'm allergic to alcohol?

No. It's a genetic enzyme deficiency, not a true allergy. A real alcohol allergy is rare and causes hives, swelling or breathing trouble — see a doctor if you get those. The everyday red-face flush is about how your body processes alcohol.
