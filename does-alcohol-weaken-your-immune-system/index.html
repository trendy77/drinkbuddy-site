Does alcohol weaken your immune system?

Reach for a nightcap when you feel a cold coming on and you might be doing your body a quiet disservice. Your immune system is your round-the-clock defence against bugs and infection — and alcohol, particularly in the amounts a big night involves, gets in its way more than most people realise.

The short answer

Yes — drinking can weaken your immune system. The US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism puts it bluntly: drinking too much alcohol can weaken the immune system, "making the body a much easier target for disease." It happens on two timescales. A single heavy session slows your body's ability to fight off infection for up to 24 hours afterwards. And drinking heavily over months and years interferes with multiple parts of the immune response at once. The good news in that is the flip side: a drink or two now and then isn't what does the damage — it's the big nights and the steady heavy pattern.

What your immune system is up against

Your immune system is less a single organ than a whole network — white blood cells patrolling your bloodstream, the lining of your gut, the defences in your lungs and airways, and the inflammation response that rushes help to where it's needed. It works best when it's well-rested, well-fed and hydrated. Alcohol chips away at several of those pieces at the same time, which is why its effect on your resistance to infection is bigger than any one mechanism suggests.

How alcohol gets in the way

1. The 24-hour dip after a big night

This is the effect most people never connect to their drinking. When you drink heavily in one sitting, your body's defences take a measurable hit. Researchers writing in The Conversation describe how, after a binge, a brief burst of inflammation is followed a couple of hours later — as your blood alcohol falls — by a drop in monocytes and natural killer cells, the white blood cells that recognise and swallow invading germs. The NIAAA notes the practical upshot: drinking a lot on a single occasion slows the body's ability to ward off infection "even up to 24 hours later." So a heavy Saturday can leave you more exposed to whatever's going around right through Sunday.

2. It disrupts your gut

A large share of your immune system lives in and around your gut, so what alcohol does there matters. Drinking irritates the gut lining and upsets the balance of the bacteria living in it — both of which play a part in keeping your defences working and keeping harmful microbes in check. If you've ever wondered why a big night leaves your stomach in a state, our guide on why alcohol upsets your stomach goes into the detail; the same irritation that causes the queasiness also undermines one of your body's front lines of defence.

3. It leaves your lungs more exposed

Your airways have their own cleaning and defence system, and alcohol blunts it. The NIAAA links alcohol misuse to a higher risk of serious chest problems, including acute respiratory distress syndrome and bacterial pneumonia, and notes that people who drink often are more prone to infections like pneumonia and tuberculosis than people who don't drink to excess. It's part of why a heavy drinking pattern and chest infections so often go together.

4. Regular heavy drinking wears defences down

Over the long term, heavy drinking does more than cause a temporary dip. It can reduce the killer T cells that hunt down and destroy infected cells — an effect the poor diet common among heavy drinkers only adds to. It also stokes the kind of low-grade, uncontrolled inflammation that, rather than helping, contributes to chronic disease. The overall result is a body that's slower to fight off infection and slower to recover and repair afterwards.

Can a drink actually fight off a cold?

It's a stubborn myth — the hot toddy, the medicinal whisky, the idea that alcohol will "kill the bug." It won't. The alcohol in a drink is far too weak to kill bacteria or viruses inside your body, and it does nothing like the job the alcohol in hand sanitiser does on your skin. Worse, a nightcap works against recovery in two everyday ways: it disrupts the deep sleep your body relies on to repair itself — covered in our guide on alcohol and sleep — and it dehydrates you, which can leave cold and flu symptoms feeling worse rather than better. If you're already unwell, the simplest thing you can do for your immune system is give the drinks a miss until you're over it.

What about moderate drinking — doesn't some research say it helps?

You may have seen older studies suggesting moderate drinkers, or wine drinkers in particular, caught fewer colds, or one much-cited study where moderate-drinking monkeys responded better to a vaccine. It's worth being honest about what that does and doesn't show. These findings are easily muddied by other factors — whether people smoke, how they live, how socially active they are — and they don't establish that alcohol itself strengthens immunity. No health authority recommends drinking for any health benefit, and the World Health Organization is clear that when it comes to health, there is no safe level of alcohol consumption. Australia's guidelines are a ceiling to stay under, not a target to aim for.

If your immune system is already under pressure — you're fighting an infection, recovering from illness, run-down, pregnant, older, or living with a condition or on medication that affects your immunity — the sensible amount of alcohol may be lower, or none. This article is general information, not medical advice, so talk to your doctor about what's right for you. If you're worried about your own or someone else's drinking, the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline is on 1800 250 015.

Drinking in a way that's kinder to your immune system

You don't have to give up drinking to protect your defences — the pattern matters far more than the occasional drink. A few habits that genuinely help:

  • Avoid big, fast sessions. The sharp 24-hour dip in your defences comes from heavy one-off drinking, so pacing yourself at about one standard drink an hour is the single most useful change.
  • Stay within the national guidelines. Australia's advice is no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day — a limit, not a goal.
  • Protect your sleep. Your immune system does much of its work while you rest, and alcohol wrecks sleep quality. Finishing earlier and lighter helps.
  • Eat and hydrate. Drink water alongside alcohol and don't drink on an empty stomach — it supports the gut and offsets the drying-out that makes you feel worse.
  • Build in alcohol-free days and skip the drinks entirely when you're unwell or run-down, when your body needs its defences most.
A note on driving: looking after your immune system doesn't change the rules behind the wheel. In Australia the legal limit for full-licence drivers is under 0.05, and zero for learner and provisional drivers. Your body clears only about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds that up — so if you've been drinking, don't drive. These figures are estimates and affect everyone differently.

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol weaken your immune system?

Yes. The NIAAA states that drinking too much can weaken the immune system and leave the body an easier target for disease. A single heavy session slows your ability to fight off infection for up to 24 hours, and regular heavy drinking interferes with the immune response over the long term. A drink or two occasionally isn't the issue — the big nights and the heavy pattern are.

How long does alcohol affect your immune system after drinking?

Drinking a lot on a single occasion slows the body's ability to ward off infection for up to about 24 hours afterwards, per the NIAAA. In that window your infection-fighting white blood cells drop, so a big night can leave you more open to catching something the next day.

Does drinking alcohol make a cold worse?

It can. Alcohol doesn't kill the cold or flu virus, and a heavy session lowers the white blood cells you need to fight it. It also disrupts the deep sleep your body uses to recover and dehydrates you, both of which can make symptoms feel worse. If you're unwell, it's usually best to skip the drinks.

Does alcohol kill germs when you drink it?

No. The alcohol in a drink is far too weak to kill bacteria or viruses inside your body, and it doesn't work like the alcohol in hand sanitiser. A hot toddy or a whisky before bed won't fight off a cold or flu, and by disrupting sleep and drying you out it can leave you feeling worse.

How much alcohol is okay for your immune system?

There's no amount that boosts immunity, and health authorities don't recommend drinking for any health benefit. Staying within Australia's guidelines — no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day, per the NHMRC — keeps the risk low. The biggest favour you can do your defences is to avoid big, fast sessions.
