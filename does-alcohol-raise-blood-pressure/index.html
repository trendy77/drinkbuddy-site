Does alcohol raise blood pressure?

Yes — drinking raises blood pressure, and the effect builds with every extra drink. The good news is that it works both ways: cutting back lowers it again. Here's what the evidence actually shows.

High blood pressure (hypertension) is one of the biggest drivers of heart attack and stroke, and it's often silent — you can't feel it. Alcohol is one of the lifestyle factors most consistently linked to it. If you're keeping an eye on your blood pressure, understanding how drinking fits in is worth a few minutes.

The short version. Drink regularly and your blood pressure tends to rise; the more you drink, the bigger the rise. A single big night has a two-stage effect — a dip for a few hours, then a rebound the next day. Reducing how much you drink is one of the more reliable ways to bring blood pressure back down.

How alcohol pushes blood pressure up

Blood pressure is the force of blood against your artery walls, written as two numbers — systolic (the higher number, when your heart beats) over diastolic (the lower number, when it rests). A reading at or above 140/90 mmHg is generally classed as high.

Alcohol affects that pressure through several overlapping mechanisms. It nudges the sympathetic ("fight or flight") nervous system into higher gear, raises levels of stress hormones like cortisol, stiffens blood vessels over time, and interferes with the hormones and kidney processes that manage fluid balance. Regular heavy drinking also tends to add kilojoules and disturb sleep — both of which push blood pressure the wrong way. The result is a rise that, unlike a temporary spike, can settle in and stay.

The two-stage short-term effect

One session doesn't move your blood pressure in a straight line. Research describes a biphasic (two-stage) response: in the first hours after drinking, alcohol relaxes blood vessels and blood pressure often dips for up to around 12 hours. After that, it swings the other way and rises — which is part of why blood pressure (and heart rate) can be noticeably higher the morning after a big night.

So a home reading taken late in the evening might look reassuringly normal, while the next-morning reading tells a different story. That rebound is the short-term face of a longer-term pattern.

It's dose-dependent — the numbers

The clearest message from the research is that alcohol and blood pressure rise together. People who have three or more standard drinks a day have roughly three times the rate of mild hypertension compared with non-drinkers. And the effect scales with the amount:

A few mmHg might not sound like much, but across a whole population even small shifts in average blood pressure translate into meaningfully more heart attacks and strokes. In Australia, a "standard drink" is 10 g of pure alcohol — smaller than most poured drinks, which is why it's easy to be drinking more than you think.

Worth knowing. The risk isn't only at the extreme end. Reviews have found that in women, the increased blood-pressure risk can begin even at moderate drinking levels — so "I only have a couple" isn't automatically in the clear.

Cutting back lowers it again

Here's the encouraging part. A large systematic review and meta-analysis published in The Lancet Public Health found that reducing alcohol lowers blood pressure in a dose-dependent way — and the more someone was drinking to begin with, the bigger the fall when they cut back. For heavier drinkers, reducing intake produced clinically useful drops in both systolic and diastolic pressure.

Put simply: if you drink more than a couple a day, cutting down is one of the more reliable non-medication levers you have. It won't replace prescribed treatment, but it genuinely moves the numbers — and it stacks with the other usual suspects (less salt, more movement, a healthier weight, better sleep).

How much is "safe" for blood pressure?

The honest answer is that there's no completely safe level — the lowest risk is not drinking at all. But for people who do drink, Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) sets an upper limit of no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day for healthy adults.

The Heart Foundation is clear that this is a ceiling, not a target — and that people with, or at risk of, heart conditions like high blood pressure may benefit from drinking less or not at all. If you've been diagnosed with hypertension, cutting back further (or stopping) is one of the most useful conversations to have with your GP.

A note on measuring. If you check your blood pressure at home, timing matters. A reading in the hours right after drinking can be misleadingly low because of that early dip, while the next morning may read higher. For a fair picture, measure when you haven't had alcohol in the previous 24 hours, and mention your drinking honestly when your GP takes a reading.

Keeping track without the guesswork

Frequently asked questions

Does alcohol raise blood pressure?

Yes. Drinking regularly raises blood pressure, and the more you drink the greater the rise. Having three or more standard drinks a day is linked to about three times the rate of high blood pressure versus not drinking.

How long does alcohol raise blood pressure for?

A single session has a two-stage effect — blood pressure often dips for up to about 12 hours, then rises the next day. Drinking regularly produces a more lasting, chronic rise.

Will cutting back on alcohol lower my blood pressure?

Yes, particularly if you currently drink more than two a day. The benefit is dose-dependent, so the heaviest drinkers who cut back the most see the biggest falls.

How much alcohol is safe for blood pressure?

There's no fully safe level. The NHMRC upper limit is 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any day; the Heart Foundation advises people with high blood pressure to drink less or not at all.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have high blood pressure or a heart condition, talk to your GP about what's right for you. Sources: Heart Foundation (Australia), The Lancet Public Health (alcohol reduction & blood pressure meta-analysis), Stroke Foundation Australia, Mayo Clinic.
