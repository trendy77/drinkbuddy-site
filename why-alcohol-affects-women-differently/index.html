Why alcohol affects women differently to men

By Trendy Publishing editorial team · Published 5 August 2026 · Sources

Pour a man and a woman of the same weight the same three drinks and they don't end up in the same place. Drink for drink, women reach a higher blood alcohol concentration, feel it sooner, and stay elevated longer. It isn't about tolerance or willpower — it's biology, and there are two clear reasons for it. Here's what's going on, and what it means for pacing yourself and driving.

The short answer

After the same amount of alcohol, a woman's blood alcohol level tends to climb higher than a man's — even accounting for body size. Two things drive it:

  • Less body water. Alcohol spreads through the water in your body. Women generally carry a higher proportion of body fat and less water, so the same alcohol is concentrated into a smaller volume.
  • Less of a key enzyme. Women produce less alcohol dehydrogenase in the stomach, the enzyme that starts breaking alcohol down before it reaches the bloodstream. So more of each drink gets through.

Put together, these mean the number of standard drinks is only half the story. The same count lands differently depending on the body it lands in.

Reason one: less body water

Alcohol is water-soluble. Once it's absorbed, it distributes itself through your total body water — the more water there is, the more diluted it becomes. On average, women's bodies are a lower percentage water than men's of the same weight, and a higher percentage fat, which holds very little alcohol.

The result is simple arithmetic: the same 10 grams of alcohol dissolved into a smaller pool of water reaches a higher concentration. This is exactly why blood-alcohol formulas treat men and women differently. The Widmark formula that most BAC estimates use includes a "body water" factor of roughly 0.68 for men and 0.55 for women — a smaller number for women, which pushes the estimated concentration up.

As Drinkaware puts it, because women tend to have less water in their bodies, alcohol becomes more concentrated in the blood — so women can feel the effects faster and reach a higher blood alcohol level than men after drinking the same amount.

Reason two: less of the enzyme that breaks alcohol down

Before alcohol ever reaches your bloodstream, some of it is broken down in the stomach by an enzyme called alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH). This early clean-up is known as "first-pass metabolism" — it's alcohol that never makes it into circulation at all.

Women produce less gastric ADH than men, so less of each drink is intercepted and more passes straight through. A landmark study in the New England Journal of Medicine measured this directly: in the women studied, gastric alcohol dehydrogenase activity was about 59% of the men's, and first-pass metabolism only about 23% of the men's. In practical terms, a larger share of every drink reaches the blood.

Stack this on top of the body-water difference and the two effects compound. Less alcohol is removed on the way in, and what does get through is concentrated into a smaller volume of water.

What it means for your blood alcohol level

Here's a rough, illustrative comparison. Take a man and a woman who both weigh 70kg and both have three standard drinks (30 grams of alcohol) over a short period. Using the Widmark formula and nothing else:

70kg personBody-water factorEstimated peak BAC
Man~0.68~0.06%
Woman~0.55~0.08%

Same weight, same drinks — and one is comfortably over the 0.05 limit while the other is under it. And this only counts the body-water difference; the enzyme gap widens the gap further in real life. These are estimates, not promises, but the direction is consistent: the same night out reads differently on a breathalyser depending on who's blowing into it.

Why "I can keep up" is a trap. Matching someone else drink for drink assumes the alcohol does the same thing in both bodies. It doesn't. A woman keeping pace with a larger male friend can be a full standard drink's worth of BAC ahead by the end of the night without touching a single extra glass.

Hormones and other factors

Body water and enzymes are the big two, but they aren't the whole picture. Hormone levels shift across the menstrual cycle, and some people notice alcohol hits harder at certain points — though the effect is individual and hard to generalise. Where you are in your cycle, whether you've eaten, how quickly you're drinking and your overall body composition all move the number. The honest takeaway is that averages describe populations, not you: your own experience over time is the better guide.

The longer-term health picture

The differences aren't only about a single night. Because women reach higher blood alcohol levels and their tissues are exposed to more alcohol per drink, research links alcohol-related harms — including to the liver, heart and certain cancers — to lower levels of drinking and shorter timeframes in women than in men. It's one of the reasons "the less you drink, the lower your risk" applies to everyone, and lands a little sooner for women.

But aren't the guidelines the same?

They are — and that surprises people. The National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) advises healthy men and women alike to have no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day. The single set of numbers reflects long-term disease and injury risk across the population, not how quickly an individual feels drunk on the night.

So the guideline being identical doesn't mean the experience is. A shared "4 in a day" limit still leaves a woman at a higher blood alcohol level than a man who drank the same four. The Alcohol and Drug Foundation stresses these are guidelines for reducing risk, not targets — and the less you drink, the lower your risk over time. Separately, the guidelines are clear that women who are pregnant or breastfeeding should not drink at all.

Don't rely on "the same as him" to drive. Standard-drink counts and BAC estimates are exactly that — estimates — and your body clears only about one standard drink an hour regardless of sex. Because women reach a higher BAC per drink, the same round that leaves a male friend under 0.05 can put a woman over it. If you've been drinking, don't drive. Only time lowers your blood alcohol concentration, and the alcohol can still be there the next morning.

What you can actually do with this

None of this means anyone has to sit out. It means counting drinks — and knowing they land differently:

  • Count your own drinks, not the table's. Keeping pace with a bigger person almost guarantees you'll be further along than they are.
  • Eat first and pace slowly. Food and time are the two levers that genuinely change how alcohol hits, especially on an empty stomach.
  • Know what a "standard drink" really is. A schooner or a generous home pour is often more than one — a standard drinks calculator takes the guesswork out.
  • Alternate with water. It slows the pace and helps with hydration.

Frequently asked questions

Why do women get drunk faster than men?

Mainly two reasons: women's bodies hold less water, so alcohol is more concentrated in the blood, and they produce less of the stomach enzyme that breaks alcohol down before it's absorbed. The same drinks reach a higher blood alcohol level.

Do women reach a higher BAC than men on the same amount?

Yes, even allowing for body size. A rough Widmark estimate for a 70kg person after three standard drinks is about 0.06% for a man and 0.08% for a woman — enough to put one over 0.05 and the other under.

Is the enzyme difference real?

Yes. A New England Journal of Medicine study found women's gastric alcohol dehydrogenase activity was about 59% of men's, with first-pass metabolism about 23% of men's, so more alcohol reaches the bloodstream.

Are the Australian alcohol guidelines different for women?

The main NHMRC guideline — no more than 10 standard drinks a week and 4 on any day — is the same for healthy men and women. It reflects long-term risk, not how quickly you feel drunk. Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding are advised not to drink at all.

Does the menstrual cycle change how alcohol affects you?

It can. Hormone levels shift across the cycle and some people feel alcohol more strongly at certain points, but the effect varies from person to person. Use your own experience as the guide.
