What's the drink-driving limit in Japan?

By Trendy Publishing editorial team · Published 29 September 2026 · Sources

Japan's drink-driving limit is 0.03% blood alcohol — measured at the roadside as 0.15 milligrams of alcohol per litre of breath. That's one of the strictest limits in the world, roughly half of Australia's 0.05, and Japan backs it with penalties that stretch into years of prison — and that can also catch the people who supplied you a drink, a lift or the car. Here's how it works, and what it means if you're driving in Japan on an Australian licence.

The limit: 0.03% BAC (0.15 mg/L of breath)

Under Japan's Road Traffic Act, it is an offence to drive with a breath alcohol concentration of 0.15 milligrams per litre or more, which equates to about 0.03 grams of alcohol per 100 millilitres of blood — a blood alcohol concentration of 0.03%. Police measure breath at the roadside, and that breath figure is what the offence is built on.

To put 0.03% in context, most of the drink-driving world sits at 0.05% or 0.08%. Australia's general limit is 0.05, England and the United States use 0.08, and Japan's 0.03 is meaningfully lower than all of them. It is not a full zero-tolerance number on paper, but in practice it is close enough that it's best treated as one.

Why the breath number matters. The 0.15 mg/L breath figure isn't a separate, looser rule — it's simply the breath-test equivalent of 0.03% blood alcohol. Different units, the same underlying limit. A breath test is what police use at the roadside; a reading at or above 0.15 mg/L is enough for the offence.

Japan has two drink-driving offences

This is where Japan differs from Australia. Rather than one offence with penalty bands, Japanese law splits drink-driving into two separate crimes.

Driving with alcohol present (shukiobi unten) is the numeric offence: a breath reading of 0.15 mg/L or more. You don't have to appear drunk — being at or over the number is the offence in itself.

Drunk driving (sakeyoi unten) is the more serious charge, and it isn't tied to a number at all. If police judge that alcohol has left you unable to drive normally — unsteady, slurring, unable to walk a straight line — you can be charged with drunk driving even without a specific reading. It carries much heavier penalties.

These are maximums, and a court decides the actual sentence — but even the lower offence can mean a criminal conviction and the loss of your licence. Japan's National Police Agency publishes the framework and runs a long-standing campaign to stamp out drink-driving entirely.

It's not just the driver who's liable

Japan's most striking feature is that the law reaches the people around the driver. After a series of high-profile fatal crashes, the rules were toughened so that supplying the car, pouring the drinks, or even taking the ride can all be offences in their own right.

The passenger rule catches travellers out. If you knowingly get into a car with someone who has been drinking — or lend them your hire car, or shout them the drinks first — you can be prosecuted too. "I wasn't driving" is not a defence in Japan.

How many drinks is 0.03%?

There's no honest number, and at this limit that matters more than usual. A single standard drink raises an average adult's blood alcohol by roughly 0.015–0.02%, so one drink can be enough to reach or pass 0.03% — and how far it takes you depends on your weight, your sex, how quickly you drank and whether you've eaten. Two people who had the same drink can test on opposite sides of the line.

Because the limit is so low and the penalties so heavy, the only sensible approach in Japan is the one the authorities intend: if you're going to drive, don't drink at all. There is no "one won't hurt" margin to work with here.

Driving in Japan on an Australian licence

Australians can drive in Japan for up to 365 days from the date of entry on a current Australian state licence together with an International Driving Permit issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention — you must obtain the permit in Australia before you travel, as it can't be issued once you've left. The Australian Embassy in Tokyo sets out the requirements, and the Smartraveller advice for Japan covers local laws more broadly.

Crucially, Japan's 0.03% limit applies to you exactly as it does to a local driver — a foreign licence gives you no leeway. And there's a financial sting beyond the fine: most travel insurance policies and hire-car agreements exclude cover if you were driving over the legal limit, so a drink-driving crash can leave you personally liable for the damage as well as facing the criminal penalty. It's a trap worth remembering on a Niseko or Hakuba ski trip, where an après-ski drink and a snowy drive back to the lodge can combine badly.

How to get home instead

Japan makes not driving easy. Its rail and metro networks are among the best in the world, taxis are everywhere in the cities, and there's one option Australia doesn't really have: the daikō (代行) service. For a fee, two drivers come to you — one drives you home in your own car, the other follows to collect their colleague. It exists precisely because the drink-driving laws are so strict, and it means you never have to leave your car behind or risk driving after a drink.

The morning after still counts

Alcohol leaves the body slowly — on average your system clears only about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds it up. Coffee, a shower, a big breakfast and a good sleep make you feel sharper, but they don't lower your blood alcohol. Because Japan's limit is so low, it's easier than you'd think to still be over 0.03% the next morning after a big night. If you drank late, do the maths before you drive — or take the train.

If you're used to Australia's 0.05, the mental adjustment for Japan is simple but important: the safe number to drink before driving is lower than you're used to, and the penalties — for you and for the people with you — are a great deal heavier.

A note on driving: no app, calculator or rule of thumb can tell you you're under a limit — let alone safe — to drive. Blood and breath alcohol estimates carry real uncertainty, and impairment begins below every legal limit. If you've had a drink in Japan, don't drive. Take the train, grab a taxi or call a daikō.

Frequently asked questions

What is the drink-driving limit in Japan?

It's 0.03% blood alcohol, measured at the roadside as 0.15 milligrams of alcohol per litre of breath — one of the strictest legal limits in the world, roughly half of Australia's 0.05% general limit.

How many drinks can I have and still drive in Japan?

There's no safe number. At 0.03% a single standard drink can put an average adult at or over the limit, and your reading depends on your weight, sex, timing and food. If you're driving, treat it as zero and don't drink at all.

What are the penalties for drink-driving in Japan?

Driving with alcohol present (0.15 mg/L of breath or more) carries up to 3 years' prison or a fine up to ¥500,000. Drunk driving — being impaired enough that you can't drive normally — carries up to 5 years' prison or a fine up to ¥1,000,000, plus loss of your licence.

Can passengers get in trouble for riding with a drunk driver in Japan?

Yes. Supplying the vehicle can carry the same penalty as the driver, and providing alcohol to a driver or knowingly riding as a passenger can bring up to 3 years' prison or a ¥500,000 fine. Not being behind the wheel is no defence.

Can I drive in Japan on my Australian licence?

Yes, for up to 365 days, with a current Australian licence plus an International Driving Permit (1949 Geneva Convention) obtained before you leave Australia. Japan's 0.03% limit applies to you fully, and driving over it can void your travel insurance and hire-car cover.
