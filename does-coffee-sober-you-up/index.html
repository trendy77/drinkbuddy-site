Does coffee sober you up?

By Trendy Publishing editorial team · Published 30 September 2026 · Sources

The short answer is no. A strong coffee can make a drunk person feel more awake — but it does nothing to your blood alcohol content. All you get is a wide-awake drunk, which is arguably more dangerous than a sleepy one.

It's one of the most stubborn myths in drinking culture: black coffee, a cold shower, a big glass of water, and you're right to go. The coffee part feels convincing because it does something real — caffeine wakes you up. The problem is that feeling alert and being sober are two completely different things, and only one of them keeps you off the road legally and safely.

Why people think coffee works

Alcohol is a depressant. It slows your brain and body down, which is why after a few drinks you feel drowsy, uncoordinated and slow to react. Caffeine is a stimulant — it does the opposite, blocking the brain chemicals that make you feel tired.

So when you drink a coffee after a big night, the sleepiness lifts. Your brain reads that as "I'm getting better." But the caffeine has only pushed back against one symptom of being drunk — the drowsiness. The alcohol in your blood, and the impairment that comes with it, hasn't moved at all.

The core problem: caffeine changes how drunk you feel, not how drunk you are. Removing the "I'm tired, I should stop" signal is exactly what makes a wide-awake drunk risky — you feel capable of things you're not.

What actually lowers your BAC

Your liver does about 90% of the work of breaking down alcohol, and it works at a fixed pace — roughly one standard drink (10 g of alcohol) per hour. That translates to your blood alcohol content falling by around 0.015% per hour. Nothing you eat, drink or do changes that rate. There is no fast-forward button.

So if you finish drinking with an estimated BAC of 0.10, you're looking at close to seven hours before you're back to zero — coffee or no coffee. Here's roughly how that plays out:

These are averages — your own rate could be slower — so treat any figure as a rough guide, never a green light. A coffee doesn't shave a single minute off these times.

The science: "a wide-awake drunk"

Researchers at Temple University in the United States tested this directly. In a study led by psychologist Thomas Gould, mice were given alcohol, caffeine, both, or neither, then set a learning task. The alcohol-impaired mice couldn't learn the task properly — and adding caffeine did not fix it. The caffeine improved alertness and reaction slightly, but it couldn't restore the impaired judgement and learning to sober levels. As Gould put it, if you give a drunk person a coffee, "all you're making is a wide-awake drunk."

A field study from the University of Florida found the same pattern in real bars. Researchers breath-tested more than 800 patrons leaving a nightlife district. Those who had mixed alcohol with caffeine were about three times more likely to be highly intoxicated (their average breath reading was 0.109, well over the driving limit) and four times more likely to say they intended to drive than people who drank alcohol alone. Caffeine hadn't made them safer — it had made them feel capable of getting behind the wheel.

Why this matters for driving. The Australian limit is under 0.05 for full-licence drivers and zero for learners and P-platers. Impairment starts below 0.05. Because coffee leaves your BAC untouched but strips away the tiredness that might have stopped you, it's the classic set-up for driving when you're still over the limit without realising it.

Mixing caffeine and alcohol on the night

The same trap applies to espresso martinis, vodka-and-energy-drinks and pre-mixed caffeinated cans. Australia's Alcohol and Drug Foundation notes that combining the two masks alcohol's sedative effects — the cue that normally tells you you've had enough — which can lead to drinking more, taking more risks and greater alcohol-related harm. You end up drinking past the point your body would otherwise have slowed you down, and the caffeine also puts extra strain on your heart and adds to dehydration.

For reference, an espresso carries around 145 mg of caffeine and an instant coffee around 80 mg. Healthy adults are advised to keep to no more than about 400 mg of caffeine a day — so leaning on coffee to "power through" a big night stacks up quickly.

What about the morning after?

Reaching for a coffee to shake off a hangover is just as ineffective as a cure. The United States National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism is blunt about it: there is no cure for a hangover other than time. Drinking coffee, taking a shower or having a "hair of the dog" won't speed your brain's recovery. Coffee may lift the grogginess for a while, but as a mild diuretic it can add to the dehydration you're already dealing with — and if you were heavily intoxicated the night before, you may still have alcohol in your system in the morning regardless of how alert you feel.

What genuinely helps instead

Since nothing sobers you up faster, the only real strategy is to not get more intoxicated than you intend to in the first place:

  • Pace yourself — around one standard drink an hour keeps you roughly in step with how fast your liver can clear it.
  • Count standard drinks, not glasses — a home pour of wine or a craft beer can be well over one standard drink each.
  • Alternate with water and eat before and while you drink to slow absorption.
  • Sort your trip home before you start — plan a lift, rideshare, public transport or a sober driver, so "I'll just have a coffee and drive" never becomes the plan.
  • Give it time — the only thing that lowers your BAC. If in doubt, don't drive, and remember you can still be over the limit the next morning.

Australia's NHMRC guidelines suggest healthy adults have no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day to keep the risk of harm low. Staying near those numbers is a far more reliable way to end the night in reasonable shape than any amount of coffee.

Frequently asked questions

Does coffee sober you up?

No. It makes you feel more awake, but your blood alcohol content and your impairment stay exactly the same. Your liver clears alcohol at a fixed rate and caffeine doesn't change it.

How long does it actually take to sober up?

Your BAC drops by about 0.015% per hour — roughly one standard drink an hour. A BAC of 0.10 takes close to seven hours to reach zero. Only time works.

Is it safe to drive after coffee if I've been drinking?

No. Coffee changes how alert you feel, not how impaired you are or what your BAC reads. If you've been drinking, don't drive.

Does mixing coffee or energy drinks with alcohol make things worse?

It can. Caffeine hides alcohol's sedative effects, so people feel less drunk than they are, drink more and take more risks — including deciding to drive.

Does coffee help a hangover?

Not really. There's no cure but time. Coffee may ease grogginess briefly, but as a diuretic it can add to dehydration and does nothing to clear the alcohol byproducts making you feel rough.
