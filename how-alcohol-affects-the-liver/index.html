How alcohol affects the liver

Your liver does almost all the work of clearing alcohol from your body — but it can only handle about one standard drink an hour. Drink faster or more often than it can keep up with, and damage quietly begins. Here's what happens, stage by stage, and what you can still turn around.

What your liver does with a drink

When you have a drink, most of the alcohol is broken down by your liver. It does roughly 90% of the job; the remaining 10% or so leaves through your breath, sweat and urine. The liver works at a fixed pace — a healthy one can process only about one standard drink (10 g of alcohol) per hour. As soon as you drink faster than that, alcohol banks up in your bloodstream and your liver is forced to work harder than normal to catch up.

The problem isn't just the volume of work — it's the by-product. As the liver breaks alcohol down, it releases a toxin called acetaldehyde, which damages liver cells and triggers inflammation. Your liver also shifts its metabolism toward storing fat while it's busy processing alcohol, which is why fat starts to accumulate inside liver cells after even a few days of heavy drinking.

You don't have to be drunk to harm your liver. Any alcohol makes your liver work harder than normal, and regular drinking above the recommended limits raises your risk — even if you never feel visibly intoxicated. The liver damage that matters most builds up quietly over time.

The three stages of alcohol-related liver disease

Alcohol-related liver disease isn't one condition — it's a progression. Australia's Liver Foundation describes three broad stages. They often follow one after another, but not always: some heavy drinkers develop cirrhosis without an obvious hepatitis stage in between.

1. Fatty liver (steatosis)

This is the earliest and most common form of alcohol-related liver damage. Fat starts to build up inside the liver cells, making it harder for the liver to work properly. It can begin after just a few days of heavy drinking. The catch is that fatty liver usually causes no symptoms — you might feel a little more tired or unwell than usual, or notice nothing at all. The good news: fatty liver caused by alcohol can be reversed. If you stop drinking, the liver can clear the excess fat, often within weeks to a few months.

2. Alcohol-related hepatitis

"Hepatitis" simply means inflammation. At this stage the liver becomes swollen and inflamed and some healthy cells die. It can come on suddenly after heavy or binge drinking (acute), or build up over years of regular heavy drinking (chronic). Severe acute alcohol-related hepatitis can be very serious and may need hospital treatment. But if it hasn't progressed to cirrhosis, the damage can still stabilise and even improve if you stop drinking completely.

3. Cirrhosis

This is the most severe stage. Long-lasting inflammation leaves the liver so scarred that it becomes hard, lumpy and shrunken, and can no longer do its job. Cirrhosis usually cannot be reversed, and it raises the risk of liver failure and liver cancer. Even here, stopping alcohol matters enormously: it's the single most important thing you can do to keep the disease from getting worse. If you've been diagnosed with cirrhosis, the safest amount of alcohol is none.

It's often silent until it's serious. Because fatty liver and early damage rarely cause symptoms, alcohol-related liver disease is frequently first picked up on a routine blood test rather than because someone felt unwell. By the time obvious symptoms appear, the disease has often already progressed.

Warning signs to know

Early on there may be nothing to notice. As the disease advances, symptoms can include:

  • Jaundice — yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes
  • Fatigue and feeling generally unwell
  • Swelling in the lower legs (oedema) or the belly (ascites)
  • Easy bruising and bleeding
  • Nausea, vomiting or loss of appetite
  • Confusion or changes in behaviour

Many of these can be mild or caused by something else — but if you're drinking regularly and notice them, it's worth talking to your doctor. A simple liver function blood test can flag problems long before you'd feel them.

How much is too much for your liver?

There's no drinking level that's completely risk-free for your liver, and risk climbs the more you drink and the more often you binge. Australia's NHMRC alcohol guidelines set a practical ceiling for healthy adults: no more than 10 standard drinks a week, and no more than 4 standard drinks on any one day. Drinking above this raises your risk of alcohol-related liver disease.

Some people are at higher risk than others at the same level of drinking — including people who are overweight, who eat poorly, who already have another liver condition such as hepatitis C, or who carry certain genes. Women are also more prone to alcohol-related liver damage than men, drink for drink.

Where tracking helps. Because your liver clears only about one standard drink an hour, the two things that protect it most are pacing (not out-drinking your liver in a session) and keeping your weekly total down. That's far easier when you can actually see the numbers — how many standard drinks you've had tonight, and how the week is adding up — rather than guessing.

Giving your liver a break

The liver is remarkably good at healing early damage when it gets the chance. A few habits that help:

  • Have several alcohol-free days a week. Regular breaks give the liver time to recover and pull your weekly total down.
  • Pace yourself. Aim to stay under one standard drink an hour so you're not constantly out-running your liver's capacity.
  • Know what a standard drink actually is. A "glass" of wine or a schooner of full-strength beer is often more than one standard drink, so it's easy to drink more than you think.
  • Eat well and keep a healthy weight. Excess body fat and poor nutrition add to the strain on your liver.
  • Get checked if you're worried. A blood test is quick, and catching fatty liver early means it can be reversed.

Frequently asked questions

How much alcohol can the liver process per hour?

About one standard drink — 10 g of alcohol — per hour. Drink faster and the excess builds up in your blood while your liver works overtime to catch up. Nothing speeds the liver up; only time clears alcohol.

Is alcohol-related liver damage reversible?

Early fatty liver can reverse if you stop drinking, often within weeks to months. Hepatitis that hasn't reached cirrhosis can also improve with abstinence. Cirrhosis — permanent scarring — usually can't be reversed, though stopping alcohol still helps prevent it worsening.

How much drinking damages your liver?

Any drinking makes your liver work harder, and you don't need to get drunk to cause harm. Risk rises with how much and how often you drink. Staying within the Australian guidelines — 10 standard drinks a week or fewer, and no more than 4 in a day — lowers that risk.

Does fatty liver from alcohol have symptoms?

Often none. You might just feel a bit more tired. That's what makes early liver damage dangerous — it builds silently, and clear symptoms like jaundice usually only show up once it has progressed.

Do alcohol-free days help your liver?

Yes. Because the liver only clears about one drink an hour and any alcohol makes it work harder, spacing drinks out and taking several alcohol-free days a week gives it time to recover.
